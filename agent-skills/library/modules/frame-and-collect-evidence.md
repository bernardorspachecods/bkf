# Frame and collect evidence

- Frame the work as a small set of material claims. A claim is material if changing its answer would change the user's decision or another material claim depends on it; when unsure, treat it as material and rank it. Write each as a testable statement with one idea, rank its importance (high: the answer rests on it; medium: it shapes qualifications; low: margins), and say what kind of evidence would answer it and how much corroboration it needs in proportion to importance. Record scope limits with their reasons, so a later reader can tell an irrelevant source from an inconvenient one. Frame for what the user needs to decide, not only what they typed, and do not search or name sources while framing.
- Collect before judging. Gather a broad body of plausible in-scope material across the planned source roles (official records, the original study or dataset, registries, standards, practitioner sources), including material that does not confirm what you expect, and filter afterwards. Spread effort across roles instead of letting one productive site absorb the search, follow citations backward and forward, and search deliberately for disconfirmation. A low-yield search is not by itself a reason to stop; state why collection stopped.
- Assess quality and support separately: a credible source may not support the exact claim, and a weak one may. Assess role, quality, and independence as separate judgments, read laterally (search about the producer, corrections, retractions, where the figures came from), and trace where the information originated.
- Count provenance clusters, not sources. Sources deriving from one study, dataset, release, or statement are one cluster, so five articles repeating one press release are one. Set a source aside only for quality or scope fit, never because it disagrees or overlaps.
- Extract exactly. Copy the passage verbatim from content you opened in this run, never from memory or a snippet, with a location precise enough to find it again. Record the access mode honestly (full text, partial, snippet only, inaccessible); a snippet-only entry is unverified and cannot carry a material claim. State what each entry establishes and what it does not (scope, period, population, method, interpretation without data).
- For an inaccessible source, try a legitimate alternate copy (the publisher's or an archived one), recorded as such; otherwise record it as inaccessible with the reason, and do not infer its content from the title, abstract, or other sources' descriptions.
- Match rigor to stakes. For a narrow lookup that one authoritative source can answer, with no expected conflict and low consequences, open the closest source, copy its wording, and answer with the citation, its date, and its limits; escalate if the source is weak, the answer is disputed, or a second look shows conflict. For safety, legal, financial, medical, security, public claims, or hard-to-reverse decisions, seek multiple independent primary or authoritative sources for each high-importance claim, recommend specialist or human review, and deliver a bounded answer rather than a confident one when evidence is inadequate.
- When a material evidence gap appears outside a plan, propose structured research and wait for the user's go-ahead; do not substitute an unstructured search.
