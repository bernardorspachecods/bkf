# Read academic sources

- Before extracting findings, record the context needed to interpret a study: its question, theoretical frame, design, data, sample or population, measures, setting, the author-reported results and limitations, and what is unclear or missing. Separate what the authors report from your inference.
- Do not call a contribution novel because the paper says so. Check the relevant literature, or mark novelty provisional until that comparison is made.
- A finding summary orients and compares studies; it is not evidence. Material claims rest on the linked evidence entries, never on the summary alone, and a synthesis given summaries without the evidence entries goes back instead of proceeding.
- Organize a literature review by the concepts, mechanisms, methods, populations, or tensions that answer the question, not one paragraph per paper. Check whether design, sample, measurement, or context explain apparent conflicts. Separate a gap supported by the reviewed literature from a topic nobody searched or could access, treat papers from one dataset or trial as one cluster, and impose no study count, date cutoff, or claim of consensus the question does not justify.
- Generate research questions from supported findings, stated limitations, and verified gaps. Each is narrow enough to answer and states the constructs, population, context, and period, what would count as an informative answer or a disconfirmation, and the feasibility risks and competing explanations. Questions are proposals, not findings.
- When critiquing a thesis, distinguish a gap supported by the reviewed literature, a gap suggested by a limitation, and a gap that is only a hypothesis; state a hypothesis only when its constructs, population, and measures are explicit enough to test.
