# Writing principles

- Produce the least reading burden that reliably supports the reader's task.
- Assume ordinary task competence; supply only the local conventions, owners, workflows, exceptions, facts, and prior decisions the reader lacks, and do not repeat general behavior in repository documents.
- State required behavior, constraints, and completion conditions explicitly, and organize around them.
- Keep supporting content only when it adds a fact, a decision distinction, or reasoning not already established.
- Ground precautionary guidance in a credible scenario and its consequence; guardrails added by habit are noise.
- Start with a direct instruction and give each hard rule its reason, so the reader can apply it to cases it does not mention. For decisions that vary by context, add a diagnostic question and its principle; explain the mechanism when the reader must generalize.
- Add an example only to clarify a hard distinction or resolve competing interpretations, and keep only its distinguishing details. A single example is copied closely, incidental details included, so label examples as illustrations of the format, not templates, or give two that visibly differ.
- Put task-defining instructions and critical constraints early, with their priority stated.
- In a long document (several hundred lines, or several independent areas), add a short navigation map near the start whose entries link to real headings and are phrased as reader tasks; a generated table of contents is not a substitute.
- At a link to detail that only some readers need, name the condition for following it. In skills and context files, keep instructions (what to do) separate from descriptions (what something is).
- Remove repeated meaning across instructions, rationale, examples, and checklists, and cut framing, filler, vague intensifiers, and promotional wording that add no operational information.
- Replace a list of cases with a general rule when it keeps the distinctions the reader needs to act.
- Omit unused sections entirely; do not leave placeholders such as "N/A".
- State current behavior, intended change, and uncertainty separately; do not turn a possibility into a fact. Separate confirmed findings from uncertainties and recommendations, and say what evidence is missing and how that limits the conclusion. When a judgment rests on uncertain or contested claims, make clear what is known, what is inferred or preferred, and what is unknown. Label the evidence source of each claim, and do not present a report as direct observation. If the evidence is incomplete, say so and limit conclusions to the supported part, and do not state exact figures or a complete history it does not support.
- Prefer plain words over inflated ones (use, to, before, if, because). Choose modal verbs by intended force: "must" for a requirement, "can" for a capability, a direct statement for an observation. Check that no swap changes meaning.
