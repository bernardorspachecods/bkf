# Security and privacy: obligations and threats for an app that holds other people's data

> **Read this first: this guide is not legal advice and needs review by a specialist before anyone acts on it.** The app holds personal data of customers and of the people they bring, possibly including minors and data that can reveal health, such as dietary needs, for people who may live in several countries. It receives files and pasted images, sends data to external providers and holds mailbox credentials. Mistakes carry legal and financial consequences and some cannot be undone. Two inputs are missing and cannot come from research: which countries the affected people live in, and the app's providers, data flows and components. The consolidated text of the GDPR and the EDPB consent guidance were not read in full (section 2). The specialist's checklist is in section 2, item 11.

Objective served: a guide that supports Bernardo and the agents building the app when they decide how personal data, files, secrets and threats are handled. Mode: focused on the priorities, at the highest depth the sources allow. Authentication mechanics belong to Identity and access, and data sent to AI providers to Applied AI, which owns that flow.

Findings are marked as source, synthesis or inference. Citations use the collector's source codes (for example A S22, B C16), and the Source key at the end gives the links.
## 1. The answer

The evidence is official and authority guidance (GDPR excerpts, EDPB, ICO, CNIL, CNPD), OWASP, NIST, ENISA and CNCS material, provider documentation and enforcement and complaint reports. It maps obligations, failure modes and control families. It gives no risk likelihood and no legal determination for this app, so the table below is a consequence ordering for the specialist, not a launch approval.

### 1.1 Decisions to settle first

**Synthesis / consequence ordering:** Resolve which people, purposes, jurisdictions, providers and data categories are in scope before choosing controls. The highest-consequence decisions are whether the app may collect and use each non-user’s data at all, whether any field is special-category data, and what child-specific rules apply. Next, design workable notice, access, correction and deletion processes for people without app accounts; define retention and deletion propagation; classify public and identity-gated resources and constrain file and public-form processing. Treat mailbox and sending-domain credentials as high-impact secrets because compromise can enable trusted-domain abuse. These are priorities for specialist review, not a legal determination or launch approval.

| Priority | Decision to make | Practice supported by the evidence | Consequence if missed |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | For each field and purpose, identify affected people, applicable regime, controller/processor role, Article 6 basis and any Article 9 condition. Decide whether dietary information reveals health in its context. | Maintain a field-to-purpose and recipient map; collect only what is necessary; document basis and special-category condition separately; assess high-risk and child-related processing before launch and after material changes. | Unlawful processing, excessive collection/disclosure, enforcement and serious exposure of health or child data. |
| 2 | Decide how non-users and minors receive notice and exercise rights when someone else submitted their data. | Plan Article 14 notice with identity/contact details, purposes and basis, data categories, recipients, relevant transfers, retention period/criteria, rights and complaint route, and source; check the ordinary one-month/first-contact/first-disclosure timing and exceptions. Provide a findable rights route that accepts informal requests; use proportionate identity and representative checks; route, track and protect responses. | People may not learn of processing or be able to access/correct/delete data; over-checking can block rights, while under-checking can disclose another person’s data. |
| 3 | Set purpose-specific retention and deletion rules across records, files, derived copies, backups and processors. | Define periods by purpose and legal need; distinguish active use from archive; automate deletion/anonymisation and recipient notifications where required; record exceptions and test propagation. | Indefinite retention, incomplete erasure, unnecessary exposure and enforcement. |
| 4 | Classify public versus identity-gated routes, files and actions, then check authorization at each object and operation. | Inventory what is intentionally public; review server-side authorization for private routes, records, file retrieval and actions, including anonymous access paths. Authentication mechanics belong to Identity and access. | Anonymous disclosure or modification of customer/companion data, or use of privileged actions. |
| 5 | Establish detection and response for security incidents involving personal data. | Maintain a way to detect and assess incidents, communicate with affected people where required, and meet applicable authority-notification duties; rehearse containment and recovery. | Delayed containment, incomplete communications or missed notification duties can compound harm and enforcement exposure. |
| 6 | Bound the public file and image intake path, including parsing and retrieval. | Allowlist needed formats; validate content and size server-side; generate filenames; store outside public paths; constrain parsing, patch libraries and use scanning/CDR where appropriate; limit retrieval and retention. | Parser compromise, malware delivery, data exposure, storage exhaustion or unauthorized file access. |
| 7 | Bound unauthenticated public forms and work triggered downstream. | Apply endpoint-specific rate, size, execution, storage and provider-spend limits; enforce validation and challenge checks server-side; monitor and alert; offer accessible alternatives to anti-automation challenges. | Spam, forged submissions, denial of service, provider charges, and exclusion of legitimate people. |
| 8 | Inventory mailbox, SMTP/API, DNS and sending-domain authority. | Use narrowly scoped dedicated credentials and least privilege; store secrets securely, limit access and logging, rotate/revoke on exposure, protect provider accounts with MFA, monitor sending and reputation, and plan a pause/recovery path. Configure and monitor SPF/DKIM/DMARC for the actual sending path. | A stolen credential can send phishing/spam as a trusted identity, harm reputation or trigger provider suspension; domain authentication alone does not stop misuse of valid credentials. |

This ordering is a **synthesis**, not a measured risk score: the app’s threat likelihood, data volumes, affected countries and architecture are unknown. The legal specialist must decide applicability and obligations before anyone acts on the map.


### 1.2 Privacy rules follow the person and processing, including data supplied by a customer

**Position:** A person need not have an account or submit their own information for data-protection rules to matter. The app must map the collection route, purpose, role and applicable law for each person and provider. The collection supports a strong EU reference framework, but not a finding that EU law applies to every person or operation.

- **Source finding — EU GDPR framework:** GDPR Articles 5, 6 and 9 set purpose, minimisation, storage limitation, confidentiality, accountability, a lawful basis, and an additional condition for special-category data. Article 14 covers information duties when data were not obtained from the person; Article 17 erasure is conditional and Article 19 can require communicating changes to recipients. Sources: **B, “GDPR text (EU baseline for the courts below)”**, Articles 5, 6, 9, 14, 17 and 19 (official EUR-Lex indexed passages, partial access); **A, S24**, Articles 6, 9, 14, 15 and 17 (official indexed passages, partial access); **A, S1**, UK GDPR Articles 9, 14, 18–19 and 44–49 (full text, UK comparator only). The UK text is not substituted for EU law.
- **Source finding — indirect collection:** WP29 transparency guidance says Article 14 applies when data come from another data subject or other third party; EDPB material says detailed information is required for indirectly obtained data. Sources: **B, “European Commission transparency guidance — data from other people”**, WP260 §§24–27; **B, “EDPB basic principles and small-business FAQ”**, FAQ §§44–45. EDPB is a regulator summary, not the statutory text.
- **Source finding — content and timing of notice:** For indirectly collected data, GDPR Article 14 ordinarily calls for the person’s identity/contact details, purposes and legal basis, data categories, recipients, international-transfer information where relevant, retention period/criteria, rights and complaint route, and the source (including whether it was publicly accessible). The usual deadline is within a reasonable period and at most one month, with first communication or first disclosure timing alternatives when applicable; statutory exceptions may apply. Sources: **C, C1**, GDPR Articles 12–14, especially Article 14; **B, “European Commission transparency guidance — data from other people,”** WP260 §§24–27; **B, “GDPR text,”** Article 14 indexed excerpts. Exact applicability, exceptions and timing must be checked against authentic text and the facts; the full EU text was not directly accessed.
- **Source finding — lawful basis and special categories:** The European Commission distinguishes the Article 6 basis from the additional Article 9 condition, and the CJEU says special-category rules may apply when sensitive information can be inferred by deduction or cross-referencing. Sources: **B, “European Commission — legal grounds and child-data safeguards”**, “Legal grounds” page §§50–78, 153–195; **B, “CJEU, C-184/20 — indirectly revealed special-category data”**, judgment §§122–128. Applying the inference ruling to a dietary note is an **analogy**, not the holding; context determines whether a field reveals health.
- **Source finding — direct non-user health-inference analogue:** In *Lindenapotheke*, the CJEU press summary says order details for prescription-only medicines could reveal the health status of the person for whom a customer ordered, even where that person was not the customer; in that case the inferred information was health data. Source: **C, C3**, press summary pp. 1–2, lines 53–67. This is a closer factual analogue to customer-submitted companion information than C-184/20, but the underlying judgment was not opened and the press summary is unofficial and non-binding (p. 2, lines 68–75). It does not mean dietary information always reveals health; the field and context control.
- **Source finding — retention/deletion practice:** EDPB design guidance calls for purpose-justified periods, default deletion or anonymisation once data are unnecessary, systematic deletion and consideration of backups/logs. CNIL similarly says periods should be set by purpose and distinct operations can have distinct periods. Sources: **B, “EDPB Guidelines 4/2019 — data protection by design and by default”**, §§2.2.2.3–2.2.2.4 and 3.5, 3.8; **A, S5**, “Les durées de conservation des données,” sections on purpose-specific periods and deletion/archive; **B, “ICO storage-limitation guidance”**, lines 44–67, 118–166 and 181–187 (UK comparator). The EDPB’s 2026 coordinated erasure report reports recurring controller problems with request procedures, anonymisation, inconsistent periods and backup deletion (**B, “EDPB coordinated enforcement report — implementation of erasure,”** report summary, pp. as recorded in collection; PDF text only surfaced in search results).
- **Source finding — access and mixed-person records:** An access response may need document extracts or whole documents to make personal data intelligible, balanced against other people’s rights; actual recipients generally must be identified when known. A first copy is generally free and need not be justified. Sources: **B, “CJEU, C-487/21 — Österreichische Datenschutzbehörde and CRIF,”** §§32–45 and operative interpretation; **B, “CJEU, C-154/21 — Österreichische Post,”** §§36–40 and press release; **B, “CJEU, C-307/22 — FT (Copies of medical records),”** §§31–38, 57–69 and operative ruling. Case access was partial/indexed for some judgments, and their facts do not determine every app scenario.
- **Synthesis / practice:** Build a field-and-flow inventory connecting each person, field, purpose, source, basis, recipient, location, retention period and deletion path. For each indirectly collected group, plan a clear notice containing the Article 14 particulars above and the applicable timing, then have the specialist verify exceptions, channel and wording against actual facts and authentic law. That is the minimum useful input for applicability and design decisions; no source determines the app’s actual bases or retention periods.

### 1.3 Children and potentially health-revealing information require separate decisions

**Position:** Do not treat “minor” as one universal consent rule, and do not assume every dietary preference is health data. The evidence supports separate tests for service audience/collection route, child’s own rights, best interests, data minimisation and possible special-category status.

- **Source finding — EU children’s rule is bounded:** GDPR Article 8 concerns consent-based processing for an information society service offered directly to a child; Member States set the age between 13 and 16. It is not a universal parental-consent rule for every record about a minor. Sources: **B, “European Commission — legal grounds and child-data safeguards,”** child safeguards §§38–49; **B, “EDPB / WP29 child-consent guidance,”** §§124–128; **B, “GDPR text,”** Articles 8–9 excerpts.
- **Source finding — outcomes show consequences, not automatic transfer:** The Irish DPC found child-facing Instagram infringements involving transparency, lawful basis, minimisation, design/default and DPIA duties and imposed a €405m fine (**C, C27**, DPC case page lines 19–30; EDPB Binding Decision 2/2022 §§1–3). The outcome is not about this app or Article 9 health data. The Irish DPC’s 2026 CHI announcement found security/confidentiality, control and disposal failures involving children’s special-category health records and ordered DPIAs and controls (**C, C37**, announcement lines 10–20, 22–29); full decision is pending. These are distinct DPC outcomes and show different risks.
- **Source finding — health-data counterweight:** DPC case summaries record complaints about health-data disclosures where an Article 9 basis was absent or unnecessary medical information was shared (**C, C28**, report pp. 35–36 and 41–42). Another DPC case found a defined employment-related Article 9(2)(b) basis on its specific facts (**C, C38**, lines 20–24). Together they show that purpose, legal conditions, recipient and necessity matter; neither case classifies dietary fields.
- **Source finding — other regimes are examples, not a global map:** Brazil’s LGPD addresses best interests, minimisation, children’s rights and sensitive health data (**A, S7**, Articles 14, 18–19 and health-data provisions); Brazil’s ECA Digital adds duties if a service is directed to or likely to be accessed by Brazilian children (**A, S27**, Law 15.211/2025, Article 1, Articles 7–8 and 26–27; ANPD page). COPPA is focused on child-directed services or actual knowledge of collecting online from under-13 children; an adult’s submission about a child to a general-audience service alone does not trigger it, while child-directed services have specific duties (**A, S13**, FTC FAQ §§46, 56–57, 275–279). Québec has its own rules, including third-party collection, minors, consent and transfer assessment (**A, S28**, §§4–8, 14, 17 and 18.3). The California, Canada, UK and Brazil entries do not establish application to this operator.
- **Source finding — conditional French health-data comparator:** CNIL guidance calls for transparent information and security appropriate to sensitivity for health-data processing, and says a DPIA is required for the majority of such processing (**A, S6**, “Exigences générales,” lines 33–42 and 52–56). This is French regulator guidance, not a statute; “majority” is not “all,” dietary details are not automatically health data, and sector-specific rules may not apply. Check whether French requirements apply to the people, operator and processing in scope.
- **Source finding — erasure where data were collected during childhood:** UK ICO guidance says that collection while someone was a child can weigh in favor of later erasure, while the right remains conditional; where a child’s and parent’s interests conflict, competence and the child’s best interests matter. Source: **C, C8**, “Erasure for children,” lines 60–82. This is UK regulator guidance and a jurisdiction-specific comparator, not a universal rule or an automatic erasure entitlement; the ICO says this guidance is under review after UK legislative changes.
- **Synthesis / practice:** Classify fields by what they reveal in context, including through combination; minimize child-related collection; decide whether the product is likely to be accessed by children in each relevant market; conduct any required DPIA/child-focused assessment; and make notices age-appropriate where applicable. If French requirements apply and processing is health-data processing, verify CNIL expectations for transparent notice, sensitivity-appropriate security and whether a DPIA is required. When reviewing later erasure requests for data collected during childhood, consider the ICO factor as a UK comparator and assess competence and best interests where parent-child interests conflict. These practices follow from the listed law/guidance and case evidence, but their exact legal trigger is jurisdiction- and product-specific.

### 1.4 Non-user rights need usable routes and proportionate safeguards

**Position:** The absence of an account does not extinguish a data subject’s rights. A usable process must balance findability, low-friction submission, verification and protection against unauthorized disclosure or deletion.

- **Source finding — informal requests and verification:** EDPB access guidance says a person need not use a formal form or cite GDPR; controllers should have a process, request only necessary identity information, assess representatives proportionately, protect the reply and prevent disclosure to unauthorized persons. Children are data subjects in their own right. Sources: **A, S26**, §§6–8, 50, 62–71, 80–87 and 157–158; **B, “EDPB Guidelines 01/2022 — Right of access,”** cited guidance passages on process, identity, representatives and deadlines.
- **Source finding — Article 14 notice route:** Where a customer supplied the data, do not wait for the companion to discover the app before considering notice. The ordinary Article 14 information includes controller/contact details, purposes and legal basis, categories, recipients, transfers where relevant, retention period/criteria, rights and complaint route, and data source; it is generally due within a reasonable period and no later than one month, or by first communication/first disclosure in the specified circumstances. Sources: **C, C1**, Article 14; **B, “European Commission transparency guidance — data from other people,”** WP260 §§24–27. Exceptions and the applicable timing alternative remain fact- and law-dependent; the full EU statutory text was not directly accessed.
- **Source finding — portal friction:** In an individual Irish DPC amicable resolution, an access requester objected to being directed from email to an online portal; the controller accepted email requests after DPC engagement, but there was no merits finding (**C, C29**, decision §§3(a)–(b), 7–9). The EDPB’s coordinated access report says optional portals can help, while exclusive form or written-only routes can be barriers; identity checks should be proportionate (**C, C36**, pp. 13–14 and 18–21). These are separate origins, with one an individual resolution and the other supervisory synthesis.
- **Source finding — verification counterweight:** An EDPB-registered erasure case found using account details already held justified but required another way to proceed when the account was hacked (**B, C22**, decision summary pp. 0–1). This is a specific enforcement example, not a universal verification method. A noyb complaint alleges excessive identity checks but is a complaint, not a finding (**B, C20**, §§2–15); a separate EDPB case digest on ID checks was not opened and is not evidence (**B, C23**).
- **Source finding — enforcement example on rights channels:** CNIL found that defective rights email addresses hindered individuals’ exercise of rights even though other channels existed. The same decision concerned exploitation of an obsolete file-upload component and also addressed component updates, testing/evaluation and controller oversight of processor security (**C, C10**, Deliberation SAN-2021-001, pp. 5–6 §§40–48; pp. 8–11 §§64–83). It is a bounded enforcement example involving a particular controller/processor and historical exploit, not evidence that this app shares those facts.
- **Synthesis / practice:** Offer an accessible channel not dependent on logging into the account, accept requests arriving through other channels, route and receipt-track them, provide plain-language acknowledgement, and verify only to the degree needed for the risk and request. Test that published contact channels work and are monitored; other available routes do not cure a defective stated channel. Confirm a representative’s authority before disclosing a child’s or another person’s record. These are supported design directions, not a mandated specific form or verification technology.

### 1.5 Files and pasted images are untrusted input through every processing step

**Position:** The threat boundary includes upload, validation, parsing, conversion/thumbnailing, storage and retrieval. A separate access-control check is needed for routes, files and actions intended to be private: public reachability can expose or modify data even if the upload parser is safe. Public acceptance alone is not the only risk; downstream processing and public exposure matter.

- **Source finding — threats and controls:** OWASP identifies parser exploits, phishing, archive/XML bombs, storage exhaustion, overwrite and public retrieval risks; it recommends allowlisted formats, independent validation, limits, generated identifiers, non-public storage, safe retrieval, scanning/sandboxing or CDR where suitable, and patched/configured libraries (**A, S17**, lines 213–217, 219–239, 243–267). NIST’s input-validation control calls for syntax, semantic, length, range and value validation before attacker-controlled content reaches interpreters (**A, S33**, SI-10, printed p. 342; indexed 5.1 text, not checked against current 5.2.0).
- **Source finding — independent technical basis:** MITRE CWE-434 and CWE-400 and OWASP upload guidance cover dangerous file types and resource exhaustion (**B, “Uploads and attachments,”** CWE-434/CWE-400 entries; **B, “OWASP File Upload Cheat Sheet”** and “OWASP Denial of Service Cheat Sheet”). NIST and OWASP are independent origins for input controls; these references do not show which components the app uses.
- **Source finding — unauthenticated exposure:** OWASP’s broken-access-control excerpt and MITRE CWE-306 describe anonymous access to routes or functionality that should require identity, with possible disclosure or modification of sensitive data and privileged actions. Sources: **B, “OWASP broken access control and MITRE CWE-306 — unauthenticated exposure,”** OWASP A01 excerpt and CWE-306 lines 51–57, 83–89. This is a bounded threat statement, not a claim that every public endpoint is defective; authentication mechanics remain owned by Identity and access.
- **Source finding — concrete parser cases:** ImageMagick advisories document attacker-controlled image parser memory corruption and a path-policy race (**A, S18**, advisory lines 269–280; **A, S19**, lines 129–152, 218–230). A CISA KEV search-result excerpt records a historic ImageMagick case (**A, S20**) but was not opened. The advisories are component-specific examples, not evidence that the app is exposed.
- **Source finding — direct enforcement evidence:** CNIL’s Deliberation SAN-2021-001 concerned an obsolete jQuery File Upload flaw used to place files/scripts on a server and extract personal data; the decision treated outdated components, testing/evaluation and processor-security oversight as material (**C, C10**, pp. 1–2 §§5–10; pp. 8–10 §§64–75). It illustrates a possible failure path, not the app’s facts or a universal upload-control prescription.
- **Synthesis / practice:** Trace each accepted format through every parser, transformation, metadata extractor, storage location and download path. Match deployed/transitive versions against advisories; isolate resource-intensive parsing; set file and archive limits; ensure links are authorized and short-lived or otherwise access-controlled; and review object/file authorization for each identity-gated operation, including attempts without an account. Inventory which routes and resources are intentionally public. Exact limits depend on product needs and architecture; authentication mechanics remain out of scope.

### 1.6 Public forms can be abused to bypass checks or consume resources

**Position:** CAPTCHA or client-side validation alone does not establish server-side control. A public submission can cause storage, parsing, email and third-party spend.

- **Source finding — abuse and impact:** OWASP API4 identifies unauthenticated resource-exhaustion and cost risks, recommending endpoint-specific rate limiting and caps on request/upload size, execution, memory, operation counts and provider spend (**A, S21**, threat/impact and mitigation sections, lines 182–184); OWASP bot/business-logic guidance recommends endpoint-specific layered controls for mail-sending and expensive actions (**A, S30**, bot §§206–239; business-logic §§372–373, 471). NIST/CISA control material adds quotas, monitoring and traffic controls, but some passages are indexed rather than full-text verified (**A, S30**, NIST SC-5 and CISA DDoS references). OWASP pages share an origin and count as one independent origin.
- **Source finding — corroborated failure modes:** An OpenEuropa issue reports replayed POST submissions despite CAPTCHA (**B, C24**, issue description); an Umbraco advisory describes client-controlled form-step/CAPTCHA bypass, including mail-sending forms (**C, C31**, lines 129–159, 165–167); a NASA VDP report was accepted/fixed for unauthenticated CAPTCHA bypass and mass spam (**C, C32**, lines 5–14, with technical detail unavailable). These are independent systems and sources, but do not quantify prevalence or establish a vulnerability here.
- **Source finding — accessible fallback:** A GitHub Community user reports an anti-abuse CAPTCHA blocked an intended report (**B, C25**, March 2024 thread); EDRi reports usability barriers to regulator complaint routes (**B, C26**, pp. 5–6). They are one-off/advocacy accounts and do not establish frequency or rights-form failure. They support testing fallback access, alongside the stronger EDPB access-route evidence above.
- **Synthesis / practice:** Validate every field and challenge token on the server; apply layered endpoint-specific rate limits and quotas at both intake and downstream operations; cap file size, processing time and send volume/spend; monitor abnormal patterns and provide a human or accessible alternate route. Choose thresholds after observing legitimate traffic. No incidence estimate for this app is available.

### 1.7 Mailbox and sending-domain authority can turn credential theft into trusted abuse

**Position:** Mailbox OAuth, SMTP/API tokens, DNS access and signing authority are distinct assets with distinct blast radii. Domain authentication helps recipients assess authorization but does not protect valid credentials from misuse.

- **Source finding — secret lifecycle and mailbox scope:** OWASP recommends fine-grained least privilege, immediate revocation and repeatable rotation (**A, S22**, sections 2, 3, 8; lines 342–343, 787–788). Google documents secure refresh-token storage and materially different Gmail scopes (**B, C16**, OAuth guidance lines 58–60, 74–80; scopes lines 135–149). Microsoft warns application permissions can reach tenant mailboxes and recommends least privilege and scope controls (**B, C17**, refresh-token lines 31–40; permission guidance lines 39–50; Graph reference lines 4200–4230). These are independent first-party provider materials, but not a complete provider-neutral secret-management standard.
- **Source finding — sending credential consequences:** AWS says compromised SES send credentials can be used for spam/phishing, harm complaint/bounce rates and cause sending to be paused; it recommends a dedicated identity, narrow scope, monitoring and rotation/revocation (**C, C33**, AWS blog lines 218–235; IAM lines 21–56, 64–83; SMTP lines 7–10, 17–21, 34–40, 87–89). This is provider-specific.
- **Source finding — domain authorization:** IETF RFC 9989 defines DMARC’s alignment of SPF/DKIM with the From domain, while noting a pass does not mean email is safe (**C, C34**, §1, pp. 6–7). Google documents current sender authentication requirements and monitoring through Postmaster Tools (**C, C35**, requirements FAQ lines 31–58; sender guidelines lines 37–53, 58–85, 275–290; FAQ lines 70–97). RFC and Google are distinct origins; thresholds and provider requirements must be rechecked at launch.
- **Synthesis / practice:** Separate credentials by purpose/environment; use minimum scopes and dedicated send identities; restrict allowed sender/recipient identities and source where possible; keep secrets out of code/logs; establish revocation and recovery steps; and monitor domain alignment, sending reputation and unusual volume. “MFA on provider/mailbox administration” is supported by CISA guidance only at search-index level (**A, S32**) and is a weaker claim than the other controls. This collection does not directly establish universal rotation intervals or the best vault for this app.

### 1.8 Providers extend the data and deletion boundary

**Position:** A provider’s product, configuration, subprocessor chain, retention and transfer terms affect the actual processing path. The examples are illustrative because no app vendor inventory exists.

- **Source finding — provider contract controls:** Resend’s DPA and security/subprocessor pages describe customer responsibilities, processor instructions, return/deletion, subprocessors, transfers and plan-specific post-send/post-termination retention (**A, S15**, DPA lines 52–58; security lines 161–179). Twilio’s current DPA/register distinguishes SendGrid products and describes subprocessor notice, request assistance, deletion and retention (**A, S29**, DPA §§6.1–9, lines 906–921; register lines 798–820). These are first-party terms; neither establishes which provider is used or independently verifies performance.
- **Source finding — conditional transfer requirements:** Brazil’s ANPD Resolution 19/2024 provides adequacy and appropriate transfer mechanisms and requires additional safeguards when international transfers involve children/adolescents, with their best interests considered (**A, S9**, Articles 1 and 7, model clauses, lines 34–36 and 545–549). Québec’s Law 25 provisions require a privacy impact assessment before communicating personal information outside Québec, an assessment of adequate protection at destination, and a written agreement for the communication (**A, S28**, §17). These apply only if the relevant law and transfer conditions are met; the Brazilian regulation’s corrected operative text and annexes must be checked.
- **Synthesis / practice:** Inventory each provider, product, fields, region, subprocessors, applicable DPA/order, retention, deletion interface and international transfer mechanism. Before a provider transfer, have the specialist assess the actual transfer chain and applicable safeguards, including Brazil’s adequacy/contractual mechanism and additional child-data safeguards or Québec’s prior privacy impact assessment, adequacy review and written-agreement conditions where those regimes apply. Test whether deletion and rights assistance work through the provider. AI-provider processing is owned by Applied AI; A, S14 is only a cross-reference and is not developed here.

### 1.9 Incident detection and breach response are part of the security boundary

**Position:** Security controls must include detecting incidents and carrying out the required response and communications. The enforcement evidence is a bounded example of failures and consequences, not a template for this app’s duties or penalties.

- **Source finding — enforcement example:** In its January 2026 FREE decision, CNIL cited inadequate baseline security/detection and incomplete breach communication, alongside failure to separate former-subscriber records no longer needed after the accounting purpose ended; the EDPB summary reports fines of €42 million and €15 million (**C, C18**, CNIL announcement lines 35–44; EDPB summary, background/decision lines 21–45). This establishes that security/detection, breach communication and purpose-ended retention failures appeared together in one enforcement outcome. It does not establish proportional penalties or the exact controls and notification duties for this app.
- **Synthesis / practice:** Include incident detection, triage, containment, recovery, recordkeeping and communications in the security design. The specialist must determine which authority and affected-person notification duties apply, their triggers and deadlines, and whether providers must support those steps; test the response path against the actual data flows and provider contracts.

### 1.10 Where sources diverge

- **Who must give parental consent:** A narrow reading of GDPR Article 8, Commission guidance and EDPB consent guidance limits the age rule to consent-based processing for an information-society service offered directly to a child; national age settings vary. Brazil’s LGPD Article 14 wording emphasizes specific parental consent for children, but the ANPD interpretive statement that reportedly recognizes other legal bases was accessible only in search results (**A, S8**) and is not relied on. Brazil’s ECA Digital adds a separate service-scope test. These are different regimes and triggers, not a single contradiction; specialist review must check current Brazil text and rules.
- **Portal convenience versus barriers:** EDPB participating authorities saw optional portals as useful and exclusive/request-format requirements as potential barriers (**C, C36**); an individual DPC portal complaint resolved after email was accepted, without a legal merits decision (**C, C29**). These do not establish that every portal is unlawful or that every offline route is required; the safer synthesis is optionality, discoverability and practical alternatives.
- **Identity checks:** A regulator case accepted checks using existing account information but required a workaround for a hacked account (**B, C22**); EDPB guidance requires proportional, case-specific checks (**A, S26; C, C36**). A complaint alleging excessive checks (**B, C20**) is not an adjudicated counterexample. The evidence favors proportionality and recovery routes, not no verification.
- **Health data and email:** IMY found transport-only TLS insufficient for one insurer’s medical email because protection ended at the recipient’s mail server (**C, C39**, unofficial English translation, pp. 1–4); this is not a rule that end-to-end encryption is always required. CJEU and regulator material treats some inferential data as special category (**B, C-184/20**; **C, C3**, *Lindenapotheke* press summary pp. 1–2, unofficial and non-binding), while DPC cases show Article 9 conditions can be valid on particular facts (**C, C38**). *Lindenapotheke* involved a customer ordering prescription-only medicine for another person and is a closer non-user analogy, but neither case classifies the app’s dietary fields; dietary data are not automatically health data.
- **Special-category case outcomes:** C28 records an unlawful disclosure outcome and a separate complaint with no formal infringement finding; C38 records a defined lawful basis in an employment case. They are useful counterweights but short case summaries, not general rules.
- **Transfer and health-data guidance is conditional:** Brazil’s child-transfer safeguards, Québec’s pre-transfer assessment and agreement conditions, and CNIL’s health-data notice/security/DPIA guidance apply only when their jurisdictional and processing triggers are met. The collected material does not establish that any of these regimes applies to this app.

## 2. Weak points and suggested next step
