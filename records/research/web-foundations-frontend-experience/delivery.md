# Frontend experience: performance, accessibility, responsive use and languages

Objective served: a guide that supports Bernardo and the agents building the app when they decide how fast, accessible, responsive and multilingual the interface must be, and how to get there. Mode: broad. Component and state structure belongs to Frontend structure, the language of outgoing messages to Email and messaging, and visual brand design is out of scope.

Findings are marked as source evidence, synthesis or inference. Citations give the source heading, section, page or numbered criterion; the Source key at the end gives the links.
## 1. The answer

The evidence is mostly standards and implementation guidance (WCAG, web.dev, MDN, GOV.UK, Unicode CLDR), a few workflow studies from healthcare, and consumer-checkout research from Baymard. It describes baselines and techniques; it does not show outcomes for staff who enter records all day, so the guide gives decisions and what to test with staff, not targets.

### 1.1 Decisions to settle first

The evidence supports setting measurable baseline criteria and then validating task-specific goals with staff. The ordering here is a synthesis of the brief's priorities, not a ranking stated by the sources.

1. **Make record-entry performance observable.** Use field Core Web Vitals as a broad baseline, but define separate measures for opening the form, typing/selecting, validation, save acknowledgement and completed-record time. Segment by phone/desktop and representative routes. Use lab runs to reproduce and compare changes, then field data to confirm effects.
2. **Design the record flow around actual call work.** Minimize unnecessary questions, support common paths, retain entries through validation and interruption, and avoid avoidable mid-entry interruptions. Whether the form should be dense or stepwise, what can be saved as a draft, and what counts as fast require observation and testing with staff doing realistic calls.
3. **Set an accessibility conformance baseline, then study sustained use.** WCAG 2.2 AA supplies verifiable requirements for keyboard use, focus, reflow, contrast, text resizing, targets, labels, errors and status. Repeated-session comfort, fatigue, workflow efficiency and compatibility with staff assistive technology require direct research; conformance alone cannot establish them.
4. **Own a complete interface-localization workflow.** Set supported locales and variants, a translation method, accountable product-side owner, reviewer roles and release acceptance criteria. The sources demonstrate human translation, human-reviewed machine translation, and hybrid approaches, but do not establish which to choose here. Apply locale data and runtime internationalization facilities for display and plural-sensitive messages; separately test input and ambiguous values.


### 1.2 Performance: broad baselines help, but task speed needs its own criteria

- **Source evidence — broad page baseline.** Core Web Vitals defines LCP for main-content loading, INP for interaction responsiveness and CLS for unexpected layout movement. The recommended “good” boundaries are LCP ≤2.5 seconds, INP ≤200 ms and CLS ≤0.1 at the 75th percentile. These are general page-level targets, not task-completion guarantees. [web.dev, “How the Core Web Vitals metrics thresholds were defined,” metric definitions and 75th-percentile thresholds](https://web.dev/articles/defining-core-web-vitals-thresholds).
- **Source evidence — measure in field and lab.** Real-user monitoring (RUM) captures actual devices, networks and populations; controlled lab tests support repeatable diagnosis. CrUX only covers eligible Chrome users on supported platforms and may attribute single-page-app route transitions to the initial page. CrUX may not represent the staff population or be available for an authenticated service. [web.dev, “Why lab and field data can be different,” field/lab distinction](https://web.dev/articles/lab-and-field-data-differences); [Chrome for Developers, “CrUX methodology,” eligibility and SPA caveats](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/crux/methodology).
- **Synthesis — measure tasks as well as page metrics.** INP reports the delay until the next painted response, not asynchronous server completion or total record-entry duration. Track the user's immediate feedback and eventual save outcome separately, alongside task time and errors/rework. Use RUM to find slow interactions and controlled traces to diagnose their cause. The source recommends field-first diagnosis and lab reproduction; the task measures are a synthesis tailored to the phone-entry priority. [web.dev, “Optimize Interaction to Next Paint,” interaction components and field diagnosis](https://web.dev/articles/optimize-inp); [web.dev, “Getting started with measuring Web Vitals,” field collection](https://web.dev/articles/vitals-measurement-getting-started).
- **Source evidence — select optimizations from the observed bottleneck.** Make critical content/resources discoverable early; remove unnecessary render-blocking CSS and startup JavaScript; route-split code that is not needed at entry; keep interaction callbacks short and let immediate UI feedback render before secondary work; avoid layout thrashing and reserve space for late content. Confirm each change in lab and field data. [web.dev, “Optimize Largest Contentful Paint,” resource discovery, render blockers and server-rendering tradeoffs](https://web.dev/articles/optimize-lcp); [web.dev, “Reduce JavaScript payloads with code splitting,” startup work and deferred chunks](https://web.dev/articles/reduce-javascript-payloads-with-code-splitting/); [web.dev, “Optimize Interaction to Next Paint,” callback and rendering work](https://web.dev/articles/optimize-inp); [web.dev, “Optimize Cumulative Layout Shift,” reserved space and field/lab differences](https://web.dev/articles/optimize-cls).
- **Synthesis — performance work has costs.** Server-side rendering can add server time and worsen TTFB; code splitting may delay a later interaction on first use; inlining reduces cache reuse; preloading too much creates contention; reserved space may remain blank. The performance change should address a measured bottleneck on the actual entry path and retain clear pending, success and failure feedback for saves. The latter is a workflow inference, not a recommendation tested by the performance sources.
- **Source evidence — populations and metrics have limits.** The 2025 Web Almanac found differences between sampled mobile and desktop websites and reported LCP-related patterns, but its public-site sample is not staff apps. Its WebPageTest setup is controlled, with fixed devices/networks and logged-out, empty-cache runs; it combines lab results with CrUX. A web-vitals GitHub issue reports one publisher's divergent field INP and repeated lab results on mid-range Android; it questions attribution but received no maintainer endorsement. These examples warrant segmentation and cautious interpretation, not dismissal of Core Web Vitals. [2025 Web Almanac, “Performance” and “Methodology”](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/performance); [GoogleChrome/web-vitals issue #455, report and maintainer response](https://github.com/GoogleChrome/web-vitals/issues/455).
- **Evidence gap — independent corroboration of optimization techniques.** The final LCP, code-splitting, INP and CLS technique additions all come from web.dev. MDN provides a separate, broader performance guide, but it overlaps at the level of general implementation advice and is not an independent trial. Per the coordinator note after collection C's accepted review, independent-origin repetition for the new technique coverage remains open. Do not present these techniques as independently demonstrated app outcomes.

### 1.3 Responsive use and phone-assisted record entry: patterns are hypotheses to test

- **Source evidence — adapt to content and task width.** Flexible layout and media queries address overflow and squeezed columns; breakpoints should occur where content needs adjustment rather than at named device sizes. Container queries can adapt a control to its containing panel, but are optional when simpler flexible layouts work. GOV.UK recommends small-screen-first layouts and responsive web as a common default, while noting audience and work context may justify device priorities. This app's brief explicitly requires phone and desktop use, so both must be evaluated. [MDN, “Responsive web design,” fluid layout, breakpoints and relative units](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Learn_web_development/Core/CSS_layout/Responsive_Design); [MDN, “CSS container queries,” containment and fallback example](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Guides/Containment/Container_queries); [GOV.UK Design System, “Layout,” screen size and grid guidance](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/styles/layout/); [GOV.UK Service Manual, “Designing for different browsers and devices,” progressive enhancement and internal-tool example](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/technology/designing-for-different-browsers-and-devices).
- **Source evidence — set a reflow floor.** WCAG 2.2 SC 1.4.10 requires vertically scrolling content to work at a width equivalent to 320 CSS pixels without loss of information/functionality or two-dimensional scrolling, except where a two-dimensional layout is essential to meaning or use. This is a conformance floor, not the ideal layout for every screen; the exception is narrow. [W3C WAI, WCAG 2.2 Quick Reference, “Reflow” (SC 1.4.10)](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/quickref/#reflow).
- **Source evidence — form shape depends on staff work.** GOV.UK suggests ordering questions for common cases, branching away irrelevant questions and starting with one thing per page; it explicitly recommends merging pages when internal users repeat and switch tasks quickly. Government-user guidance cautions that staff may need to see information together and optimize for task completion. These are related GOV.UK guidance sources, not independent experiments. [GOV.UK Service Manual, “Structuring forms,” lines 95–133](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/design/form-structure); [GOV.UK Service Manual, “Services for government users,” lines 84–117](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/design/services-for-government-users).
- **Source evidence — calls and task switching occur in comparable work.** A time-motion study of 15 emergency clinicians recorded phone conversation paired with data entry among common multitasked electronic-record tasks, with longer entry tasks associated with multitasking. A separate observational study of 145 nursing shifts recorded frequent interruptions and associations between task switching, task time/difficulty and mental workload. A third prospective study observed no significant charting-time difference between typed and voice recognition entry, despite different interruption rates and correction work. These studies show comparable staff work can be fragmented; clinical settings do not establish this app's best layout or cause-effect rules. [Applied Clinical Informatics, “Characterizing Multitasking and Workflow Fragmentation in Electronic Health Records among Emergency Department Clinicians,” methods/results and conclusion](https://doi.org/10.1055/s-0041-1736625); [BMC Nursing, “Workflow interruption and nurses’ mental workload in electronic health record tasks,” results and discussion](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-023-01209-9); [Western Journal of Emergency Medicine, “Typed Versus Voice Recognition for Data Entry in Electronic Health Records,” results and conclusion](https://westjem.com/original-research/typed-versus-voice-recognition-for-data-entry-in-electronic-health-records.html).
- **Synthesis — assess workflow outcomes together.** Observe call-based entry with realistic records, staff experience levels and interruption patterns. Compare candidate question order and density using task completion time, missed/incorrect data, rework, interruption recovery and staff-reported effort. This follows the sources' research advice and observed fragmentation; the metrics and comparison plan are an inference for this app, not a published universal target.
- **Source evidence — mobile entry adds typing and overview costs.** Baymard's 2015 benchmark of 50 major mobile e-commerce sites, with 5,200 manually assigned usability scores, identifies touch-keyboard typing as slow and error-prone, reduced page overview, redundant input, poor field-description/help placement and long dropdowns without autocomplete as usability problems. Appropriate mobile input features may reduce fiddling. These findings suggest testing typing burden, visible context, repeated entry and help placement on phones; the setting is consumer checkout, the evidence is older, and it does not establish outcomes in staff record entry. [Baymard Institute, “The State of Mobile Checkout & Form Usability,” published 2 December 2015, lines 117–120, 164–174 and 201–203](https://baymard.com/research-articles/mobile-ecommerce-checkout-forms).
- **Source evidence — field count and step count are different burdens.** Baymard's 2024 checkout research argues that the number of fields users must consider may matter more than the number of steps; its reported deterioration beyond eight steps is specific to checkout and must not become a universal threshold. For this app, compare the information-entry burden and context switching across candidate flows, rather than optimizing for screen count alone. [Baymard Institute, “Checkout Optimization: Minimize Form Fields,” published 26 June 2024, lines 122–124 and 149–159](https://baymard.com/research-articles/checkout-flow-average-form-fields).
- **Source evidence — reduce re-entry and interruption.** After validation failure, retain entered values, show a focused error summary linked to invalid fields, and give matching field-level recovery guidance. Avoid validation that interrupts incomplete entry unless research shows it helps. `autocomplete` can suggest familiar personal data; `inputmode` can request a virtual keyboard but neither guarantees a value nor validates it. GOV.UK date fields are UK-oriented; three labeled fields, accepting month names, and no automatic focus movement are examples for dates users know, not universal rules. [GOV.UK Design System, “Recover from validation errors,” lines 85–97 and 125–141](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/patterns/validation/); [GOV.UK Design System, “Error summary,” “How it works”](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/error-summary/); [MDN, “autocomplete,” user-agent prefill](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Reference/Attributes/autocomplete); [MDN, “inputmode,” virtual keyboard hint](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Reference/Global_attributes/inputmode); [GOV.UK Design System, “Date input,” usage limits and field behavior](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/components/date-input/).
- **Limit — task-specific criteria remain unmeasured.** No collected source sets a universal completion-time target, decides draft/persistence behavior through phone interruptions, or evaluates the target staff workflow. Usability sessions are needed before setting thresholds or fixing the form pattern.

### 1.4 Accessibility for staff using the interface throughout the day

- **Source evidence — conformance requirements provide a testable baseline.** WCAG 2.2 includes keyboard operation without traps, visible and unobscured focus, adaptable content, contrast and text resizing, reflow, minimum target size, alternatives to dragging, page language, consistent navigation/help, labels/instructions, text error identification and status messages. SC 2.5.8's minimum target size is 24×24 CSS pixels subject to exceptions. Techniques listed by WAI are examples; criteria themselves define conformance. [W3C WAI, “How to Meet WCAG 2.2 — Quick Reference,” criteria groups and technique caveat](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/quickref/); [W3C WAI, “Forms Tutorial,” overview and sections on labels, grouping, instructions, validation and notifications](https://www.w3.org/WAI/tutorials/forms/).
- **Synthesis — include repeated-use costs in verification.** Check sticky headers/footers against focused controls, repeated navigation consistency, form errors/status announcements and pointer target size in the working interface. Automated checks supplement manual keyboard, zoom and assistive-technology testing; neither component reuse nor a checklist proves accessibility. [WCAG 2.2 Quick Reference, “Focus Not Obscured” (SC 2.4.11), “Target Size Minimum” (SC 2.5.8), “Consistent Help” (SC 3.2.6)](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/quickref/); [GOV.UK Service Manual, “Making your frontend accessible,” manual/AT testing and progressive enhancement](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/technology/accessibility-for-developers-an-introduction).
- **Source evidence — custom widgets need pattern-level keyboard behavior.** For any custom widget, follow the relevant WAI-ARIA Authoring Practices Guide pattern alongside native HTML and normative WCAG/ARIA requirements, then test it with the target browser and assistive-technology combinations. APG gives practical keyboard conventions beyond the normative specifications, but its examples are illustrative, not production-ready; APG also records known gaps, including some macOS-specific keyboard guidance. [W3C WAI, “ARIA Authoring Practices Guide: Introduction,” informative status, keyboard conventions and example limitations](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/about/introduction/).
- **Source evidence — involve disabled staff with their own access setup.** GOV.UK research guidance recommends asking participants about access needs, inviting use of their own assistive technology and settings, and avoiding assumptions about capability or feelings. The AFB report, a 2021 voluntary survey of 323 employed blind, low-vision or deafblind adults plus 25 interviews, reports workplace form/AT barriers, lost productivity and difficulty combining screen-reader listening with a phone conversation for some participants. The report is disability-focused but not representative of all disabilities or this staff group. [GOV.UK Service Manual, “Running research sessions with disabled people,” participant guidance](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/user-research/running-research-sessions-with-people-with-disabilities); [American Foundation for the Blind, “Technology and Accommodations: Employment Experiences…,” methods/limitations and phone/efficiency findings, PDF pp. 6–9, 50, 79–80](https://www.afb.org/sites/default/files/2022-01/AFB_Workplace_Technology_Report_Accessible_FINAL.pdf).
- **Source evidence — work organization and structural navigation matter for sustained use.** HSE says screen-work break needs depend on work mix, and mentions changing activity before fatigue and short frequent breaks; it gives no one-size timing rule. In WebAIM's July–August 2026 Screen Reader User Survey #11, headings were respondents' first way to find content on a long page for 67.8%, and 88.3% found heading levels very or somewhat useful. Preserve logical heading levels and landmarks so staff can scan and navigate long screens with assistive technology, and test this in the interface. The survey had 1,780 valid responses but was uncontrolled and self-selected, so these results are not population estimates or a guarantee of any individual's navigation behavior. [UK Health and Safety Executive, “Should VDU users be given breaks?,” guidance and limits](https://www.hse.gov.uk/contact/faqs/vdubreaks.htm); [WebAIM, “Screen Reader User Survey #11 Results,” methods at lines 19–27 and navigation findings at lines 299–346](https://webaim.org/projects/screenreadersurvey11/).
- **Inference — define two acceptance layers.** First, require applicable WCAG 2.2 AA criteria and verify across the actual browser/assistive-technology combinations staff use. Then evaluate repeated sessions with disabled staff for effort, fatigue, task completion and recovery. The sources support the distinction, but do not set a sustained-comfort threshold or break schedule.
- **Limit — “all-day accessible” remains an evidence gap.** Conformance does not prove comfort or reduce fatigue across a workday. Direct research relevant to sustained use, including disabled staff and actual assistive-technology configurations, is needed before the app can set those experience criteria.

### 1.5 Interface languages, translation ownership and locale behavior

- **Source evidence — identify language and direction with the text.** Set UTF-8, declare the page language with an appropriate BCP 47 `lang` tag, mark language changes on nested content, and use `dir="rtl"` for right-to-left pages. Keep visible navigation to localized versions; use local name, address, time and date formats; check examples and images for translatability and cultural bias; keep source text concise and avoid composing sentences from fragments. W3C presents these as quick tips, not a complete specification. When natural-language strings move between systems, carry their language and direction metadata from producer to consumer, preferably using metadata rather than heuristics; isolate inserted mixed-direction names or labels with HTML `dir`/`<bdi>` handling so surrounding text is not reordered. The W3C Strings on the Web source is a First Public Working Draft dated 16 July 2026, explicitly warns of standards gaps, and is not a settled interoperability requirement; treat its metadata model as a design consideration and test RTL and mixed-script content with users. [W3C, “Internationalization Quick Tips for the Web,” quick tips](https://www.w3.org/International/quicktips/index); [W3C, “Language tags in HTML and XML,” BCP 47 and `lang`](https://www.w3.org/International/articles/language-tags/index.en); [W3C Internationalization Working Group, “Strings on the Web: Language and Direction Metadata,” status/limitations, recommendations and examples](https://www.w3.org/TR/string-meta/).
- **Source evidence — language-selector ordering is a choice.** A W3C discussion records that alphabetical order changes by language and that ordering by English language name is not neutral; BCP 47 subtag order is one predictable but imperfect example, not a recommendation for this app. Choose and test the ordering with users of the supported languages. [W3C i18n-discuss issue 20, “How to order items in a multilingual list of languages?,” lines 164–165](https://github.com/w3c/i18n-discuss/issues/20).
- **Source evidence — formatting is locale data, not string substitution.** Unicode CLDR defines localized number/currency patterns, grouping, symbols and spacing; currency display may use symbols, ISO codes or names, with narrow symbols potentially ambiguous. It recommends echoing ambiguous parsed input in formatted form before use. Currency decimal-place and rounding rules are separate supplemental currency data, not locale-specific number-format data, so locale formatting does not determine the record's currency precision or rounding policy. CLDR date patterns cover different widths, grammatical month forms, calendars and numbering systems; calendar year differs from week-year. JavaScript `Intl.DateTimeFormat`, `Intl.NumberFormat` and `Intl.PluralRules` expose locale-aware behavior. [Unicode CLDR UTS #35, “Numbers,” currency and number patterns and supplemental currency data](https://unicode.org/reports/tr35/tr35-numbers.html); [Unicode CLDR UTS #35, “Dates,” pattern construction and calendar fields](https://unicode.org/reports/tr35/tr35-dates.html); [MDN, “Intl,” locale negotiation and formatting APIs](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/JavaScript/Reference/Global_Objects/Intl).
- **Synthesis — keep display, parsing and stored meaning distinct.** Use locale rules for display and explicit locale-aware parsing/validation for user input; do not infer the underlying currency or silently reinterpret ambiguous dates from formatting conventions. The CLDR and framework sources document format/input distinctions, while this separation is the practical inference needed for safe records.
- **Source evidence — complete messages need plural and context support.** ICU MessageFormat supports language-dependent plural categories and selection branches; it recommends complete sentences in branches so translators retain context. Library guides show equivalent capabilities in next-intl and i18next, and caution that runtime/polyfill support and framework defaults vary. [ICU User Guide, “Formatting Messages,” plural/select arguments](https://unicode-org.github.io/icu/userguide/format_parse/messages/); [next-intl, “Rendering translations,” plural/select](https://next-intl.dev/docs/usage/translations); [i18next, “Formatting,” Intl-backed formatters and polyfill note](https://www.i18next.com/translation-function/formatting); [Rails I18n guide, pluralization and per-request locale](https://guides.rubyonrails.org/i18n.html).
- **Source evidence — several translation workflows are documented.** The European Commission describes human translation with bilingual revision, separate monolingual review, terminology and risk-based checks. Canada's Translation Bureau describes human review of AI translation, with routine/high-impact distinctions and confidentiality controls. Mozilla documents translation memories and machine pretranslation with human approval, terminology/style guides, linguistic and technical review, in-context testing and feedback stewardship. W3C ITS 2.0 carries context, terminology, provenance, quality and translation-status metadata; it permits human and software actors but does not select one. These are examples/guidance, not comparative app-quality trials. [European Commission DGT, “Translation quality,” review and risk controls](https://translation.ec.europa.eu/languages-and-translation-european-commission/translation-quality_en); [Translation Bureau of Canada, “Using artificial intelligence for translation purposes,” review and confidentiality guidance](https://www.canada.ca/en/translation-bureau/artificial-intelligence.html); [Mozilla, “Pretranslation” and “Using l10n resources while translating,” human approval and shared resources](https://mozilla-l10n.github.io/localizer-documentation/tools/pretranslation.html); [Mozilla, “Testing localization of Mozilla projects,” review/testing flow](https://mozilla-l10n.github.io/localizer-documentation/products/l10n_testing.html); [W3C, “Internationalization Tag Set (ITS) 2.0,” localization notes, provenance and quality](https://www.w3.org/TR/its/).
- **Synthesis — own localization as a release workflow.** Name an accountable product-side owner; prepare terminology and contextual notes; keep component and dynamic UI strings localizable; record translation/revision provenance; review language and technical behavior in context; test expansion, truncation, missing strings, plural branches, directionality and locale formats; run release completeness checks. GOV.UK Frontend explicitly leaves translations to the service team and includes component/dynamic text; Mozilla and Microsoft show in-context language and technical review; i18next/Rails show stack-specific missing-key checks. These sources support the workflow elements, but not an app-specific staffing model or threshold. [GOV.UK Frontend, “Localise GOV.UK Frontend,” text ownership and component strings](https://frontend.design-system.service.gov.uk/localise-govuk-frontend/); [Microsoft Learn, “How to perform localization testing,” linguistic/in-context criteria](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/globalization/testing/how-to-perform-localization-testing); [i18next, “Missing keys,” runtime coverage limits](https://www.i18next.com/overview/configuration-options); [Rails I18n, “Handling Missing Translations,” test checks](https://guides.rubyonrails.org/i18n.html).
- **Divergence / unresolved choice — no source selects the method for this app.** The European Commission's model describes human translation and layered review; Canada's guidance allows controlled machine translation for routine content with human review; Mozilla documents human-approved machine pretranslation. These are different operating models and are not evidence that one yields better outcomes for this service. ITS 2.0 provides workflow metadata, not a choice or quality threshold. The supported locales, method, accountable owner, reviewer roles, risk levels and release-level acceptance threshold remain open decisions for the service team, informed by language users and content risk.
- **Limit — standards, library examples and falsehood lists are bounded.** Locale standards and APIs do not define storage, timezone, app currency policy, currency precision/rounding for a record type or safe free-form parsing. Falsehoods lists about names, addresses and time are practitioner counterexamples, not complete schemas or representative studies. CLDR issue tracker searches failed to provide readable issue text, so no ticket-level edge-case claim is included.

### 1.6 Where sources diverge

- **Form density and flow:** GOV.UK's “one thing per page” is a starting pattern for focus/mobile/error recovery, while its internal-service guidance allows combining information for repeat/switch work. Baymard's mobile checkout findings flag typing, overview, redundant input and help-placement costs, while its 2024 checkout counterweight says field burden can matter more than screen count. These sources concern different contexts and are not comparative staff trials; the eight-step observation is checkout-specific. Assess the actual information burden and staff workflow before choosing density or flow.
- **Performance metric confidence:** Core Web Vitals offers repeatable thresholds and broad field measurement, while the web-vitals issue documents one publisher's difficulty attributing INP and inconsistent lab results. The issue is a limited counterexample, not validation against the metric, so use field and task data with device segmentation rather than adopting either view alone.
- **Translation method:** Human revision, human post-editing of machine output and hybrid pretranslation are all documented. The sources establish viable processes and controls, not a winner or this team's ownership. Keep the choice open until users, language coverage, confidentiality and operating capacity are known.
- **Accessibility claims:** WCAG conformance is normative and testable; AFB and HSE address real workplace barriers/fatigue that WCAG does not measure. These sources answer different questions. A WCAG pass cannot stand in for sustained-use research.
- **Responsive priority:** Responsive/mobile-first guidance is a common pattern, while GOV.UK's internal-office example says another service deprioritized small screens based on its own audience. That example does not weaken this brief's explicit phone-and-desktop requirement; it shows priorities should follow actual users.
- **Language-list ordering:** W3C's discussion shows that alphabetical ordering varies across languages and English-name ordering is not neutral, but offers no universal policy. The app's supported languages and users must guide the choice.

The brief assigns outgoing message and template language elsewhere; component and state structure belongs to Frontend structure; visual brand design is out of scope.

## 2. Weak points and suggested next step

- No source establishes a universal task-time target, backend/API latency budget, or production performance budget for this app. Performance technique coverage is concentrated in web.dev; the MDN material is general and does not independently demonstrate outcomes. The coordinator accepted collection C with this independent-origin corroboration gap explicitly left open.
- The phone-call workflow studies concern healthcare, not this app's staff. Baymard's mobile form findings concern consumer e-commerce checkout; the 2015 benchmark and 2024 field-count/step-count counterweight are not direct evidence for staff records, and the eight-step observation is checkout-specific. Together they support testing typing, overview, redundancy, help placement and information burden, but do not settle question order, draft behavior, interruption recovery, input method or speed thresholds.
- Evidence for all-day use does not quantify fatigue or define comfort criteria. AFB focuses on blind/low-vision/deafblind U.S. workers and is voluntary; HSE advises work organization rather than UI design. Include disabled staff in repeat-session evaluation.
- Translation method, organizational owner, supported language variants, risk classification and release acceptance threshold remain undecided. Institutional examples are not direct trials of this app, and the collection does not establish the team's capacity or confidentiality constraints. The language/direction metadata recommendation is from a W3C First Public Working Draft, not a final standard; language-selector ordering has no settled universal answer in the collected evidence.
- Locale standards do not settle data storage, timezone rules, currency choice, ambiguous-input parsing or all country-specific name/address requirements. A CLDR issue-tracker attempt produced no readable issue; no issue-level result can be reported.
- Sources are a modest, non-systematic collection. Much of the material is standards or implementation guidance rather than app outcome research. Related sources and shared data origins must not be counted as independent repetition.
- The European Accessibility Act source was collected in a full directive reproduction with independent Commission scope corroboration. Neither establishes whether this staff app is legally in scope; do not infer applicability from the source title or summaries.

**Left out of the digest, flagged by the digest reviewer.** The digest escalated twice at the round cap and was accepted by the user with five details not carried in: WCAG 2.2 criterion 3.3.7 Redundant Entry (information already entered in the same process is auto-populated or selectable, with exceptions), relevant to fast record entry; a reported iOS keyboard and viewport issue in WebKit, an older visual-viewport resize report and an unreadable Chromium issue page, as leads to reproduce on supported phones; the practice of setting a performance budget (MDN), as a team-set budget and not a sourced threshold; the one end-user evaluation comparing raw and lightly post-edited machine translation, which found light post-editing improved acceptability for instructional content and limits generalization by language, content and sample size; and EN 301 549's procurement scope, which the source does not establish as applying to this staff app. The applicable legal and procurement standard needs verification.

**Suggested next step (the writer's inference from the weak points).** The open questions are about this app's staff, not about the web in general: what task time counts as fast, how the record form performs on real calls, and which translation method and owner the team can sustain.
