# Applied AI: reading inputs and filling records, with limits and safeguards

> **Personal data goes to external providers here, so the rules in the Security and privacy guide apply, and its specialist review should cover this flow before anyone acts on this guide.** This guide covers the AI-specific part: what the provider does with the data, how to send less, and what the model must not decide. It is not legal advice.

Objective served: a guide that supports Bernardo and the agents building the app when they decide where AI is used, what it must never decide, and how its output and data flows are controlled. Mode: focused on the priorities. General data protection obligations belong to Security and privacy, tests of non-AI code to Testing, and the choice of a model or vendor is out of scope.

Findings are marked as source, synthesis or inference. Citations give the source and section; the Source key at the end gives the links.

## 1. The answer

The evidence is provider documentation and terms, regulatory guidance, OWASP material, and research on extraction, review and evaluation, mostly from adjacent domains (correspondence, documents, clinical and contact-center studies). No opened study measures pasted multilingual conversations and screenshots against this app's schema, so the guide gives uses, limits and safeguards, not performance figures.

### 1.1 Decisions to settle first

**Synthesis (inference from the collected evidence):** Treat AI as an assistant that proposes grounded fields, uncertainty or missingness, duplicate candidates, and reply drafts. It must not be the authority that sets prices, merges or writes records, or sends messages. Enforce those boundaries in application code and require a person to confirm proposed values and separately authorize record writes and reply sends. Before sending any input to a provider, keep prices outside the model's view through an application-controlled data path; prompt instructions alone do not establish that boundary. None of these controls has been shown to work in this app yet.

The next decision is whether the product can enforce this separation in the request and action paths. Then evaluate the actual text and screenshot workflow using labelled, representative cases. External benchmarks establish plausible failure modes and evaluation methods, not this app's accuracy or acceptable thresholds.

### 1.2 Views

#### 1. AI can structure proposals; structure is not correctness

- **Source finding:** OpenAI Structured Outputs constrains successful responses to a supported JSON Schema, but explicitly warns that outputs can still contain mistakes or hallucinate when input is unrelated; refusal can also arrive outside the requested schema. Google likewise advises application-side validation for semantically incorrect schema-compliant output. (OpenAI, “Structured model outputs,” “Structured Outputs vs JSON mode,” “Handling user-generated input,” “Handling mistakes,” and refusal handling; Google, “Structured outputs,” “Best practices” and “Limitations.”) [OpenAI docs](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/structured-outputs) · [Google docs](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/structured-output)
- **Synthesis:** A schema can make a prefill easier to inspect and process, but it cannot prove a value is present in the source, complete, correctly normalized, or safe to apply. Handle refusals and incomplete responses explicitly; validate schema and semantics separately; show evidence and uncertainty with each proposed field; require human confirmation before writing.
- **Source finding:** OpenAI warns that vision can misread small, rotated and non-Latin image text. Anthropic notes that image processing may downscale images and that visual PDF handling differs from text extraction. XFUND covers multilingual forms, including Portuguese, while DocVQA is document-image question answering with a single-span answer rather than multi-field record completion. (OpenAI, “Images and vision,” “Limitations”; Anthropic, “Vision,” “Image limits and costs,” and “PDF support,” “Document processing modes” and “Key limitations”; XFUND, dataset description; DocVQA, “Task” and “Images and Questions.”) [XFUND](https://aclanthology.org/2022.findings-acl.253/) · [DocVQA](https://site.docvqa.org/datasets/docvqa)
- **Source finding:** CC-OCR reports language variation, rotation-related degradation, repeated outputs, invented values and unwanted date normalization across its tested multilingual image tasks. MailEx reports persistent difficulty with conversation history, non-contiguous triggers and non-named arguments. Entity Exchange reports large performance variation depending on how a value is introduced or revised; prompting improved results in that study but did not remove the task's limits. (CC-OCR, “language composition and metrics,” “multilingual variation,” “orientation failures,” “repetition hallucination,” and “added invented values”; MailEx, dataset/tasks/challenges; Entity Exchange, dataset/model scope/results.) [CC-OCR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.02210) · [MailEx](https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.801/) · [Entity Exchange](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-industry.132/)
- **Source finding:** A 2026 hybrid VLM-OCR study found semantic hallucinations clustered around invoice/receipt template keywords, amount-like tokens and dates in selected receipt/form stress cases, and tested confidence-guided escalation. For one model, 94.6% of its specified semantic-hallucination cases contained amount-like tokens, averaging 0.48 unsupported amount tokens per case. This is an average within that model's hallucination subset, not an overall error rate; the study does not establish app performance or gains from escalation here. (Study, “Scope and methods” and reported error concentration.) [Study](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10032-026-00620-7)
- **Limits and divergence:** These sources cover forms, document images, email or spoken contact-center conversations, not pasted multilingual conversations and screenshots in this product. XFUND establishes language/form coverage, not chat extraction quality. Benchmark scores and failure cases vary by task and input; they cannot be transferred as expected app accuracy. MailEx and Entity Exchange PDFs were not opened, so claims from those papers are limited to their accessible abstracts and publication pages.
- **App boundary (inference):** AI may propose field values and mark values as missing or uncertain. Do not equate a required schema key, confidence score, or fluent output with evidence of correctness. Preserve source snippets or visual anchors where feasible and have a person confirm before saving.
- **App boundary (inference):** Because amount-like values and dates were concentrated in the cited study's selected hallucination cases, treat extracted amounts as especially review-sensitive. Keep prices outside model input where feasible; where a source must be processed, capture evidence and route low-confidence or otherwise flagged amount/date proposals for human verification. Confidence-guided escalation is a study-tested approach, not a validated app safeguard, and must not replace source checking.

#### 2. Tools and untrusted content require application-side controls

- **Source finding:** OpenAI, Anthropic and Google describe tool calls as structured requests whose execution belongs to the application. OpenAI advises validation near side-effecting functions and explicit human approval for ambiguous or high-risk actions. OWASP identifies prompt injection through email, attachments and hidden image/document text, says filtering and labeling are incomplete defenses, and recommends limiting privileges and treating model output as untrusted. (OpenAI, “Function calling,” “Strict mode,” and “Guardrails and human review,” “Workflow boundaries matter”; Anthropic, “How tool use works,” “User-defined tools” and “The agentic loop”; Google, “Function calling with the Gemini API,” “How function calling works”; OWASP Prompt Injection Cheat Sheet, indirect-injection examples, implementation pipeline and test limits.) [OpenAI function calling](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/function-calling) · [OpenAI approval guidance](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/agents/guardrails-approvals) · [Anthropic tool use](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/agents-and-tools/tool-use/how-tool-use-works) · [Google function calling](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/function-calling) · [OWASP cheat sheet](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/LLM_Prompt_Injection_Prevention_Cheat_Sheet.html)
- **Source finding:** OWASP LLM07 says prompts are not secrets or reliable controls and recommends keeping sensitive information in systems the model cannot directly access. Its Verification Standard calls for server-side construction, schema and language checks, untrusted-input/output handling, least-privilege tool access, restricted egress and approval for sensitive actions. These are recommended controls, not empirical proof that a particular implementation works. (OWASP LLM07, “Do not treat prompts as secrets or controls” and “separate sensitive data”; OWASP LLM Verification Standard, “Secure integration and schema/language validation” and “Tool access, egress restrictions and approval.”) [OWASP LLM07](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm072025-system-prompt-leakage/) · [OWASP Verification Standard](https://owasp.github.io/www-project-llm-verification-standard/LLMSVS-v2.0-en.html)
- **App boundary (inference):** Treat pasted content and screenshots as untrusted data. Do not expose record-write, merge, pricing, or message-send tools to extraction/drafting calls. Have the application validate permissions and arguments, then require a separate explicit user action before any write or send. Price decisions and calculations belong in deterministic application logic. If input contains price figures, redact them before request construction or use a path that does not expose them to the model; inspect actual provider payloads and measure residual leakage. No collected source demonstrates that this app has implemented or verified these safeguards.

#### 3. Duplicate and missing-field suggestions remain unproven for this product

- **Source finding:** The COLING entity-matching study compares strategies across eight datasets and reports that pairwise matching can miss global consistency. BPID reports unsatisfactory F1 for even its best tested LLM and false-positive matches by both LLM and non-LLM methods for similar identity profiles. The duplicate-evaluation tutorial notes that fuzzy matches arise from typos, OCR, language, missing or outdated values, and that suitable precision/recall depends on domain and error costs. (Wang et al., “Match, Compare, or Select?,” problem framing/evaluation; BPID, PDF pp. 4–5, evaluation and false-positive example; Panse et al., tutorial, pp. 1–2.) [COLING study](https://aclanthology.org/2025.coling-main.8/) · [BPID](https://aclanthology.org/anthology-files/anthology-files/pdf/emnlp/2024.emnlp-industry.40.pdf) · [Duplicate evaluation tutorial](https://hpi.de/fileadmin/user_upload/fachgebiete/naumann/publications/PDFs/2021_panse_evaluation.pdf)
- **Source finding:** The reports from Paperless-ngx do not close the app-specific gap: issue #1688 does not confirm an actual false positive, and discussion #1992 determines the suspected duplicate was a true file-hash duplicate. These are document-ingestion tracker reports, not evidence of semantic duplicate matching in this app. (Paperless-ngx issue #1688, report and follow-up; discussion #1992, resolution.) [Issue #1688](https://github.com/paperless-ngx/paperless-ngx/issues/1688) · [Discussion #1992](https://github.com/paperless-ngx/paperless-ngx/discussions/1992)
- **Unresolved limit (review escalation):** Collector b's second review explicitly escalated the repeated gap, and the coordinator directed that it remain open: there is no demonstrated performance or source-backed safeguard for missing-field detection or duplicate matching on this app's conversations and screenshots. No public benchmark was identified for these exact tasks. The proposed local evaluation below is a method, not evidence of efficacy.
- **Proposed evaluation (not a finding):** Create a consented or appropriately de-identified representative, held-out set stratified by language, text/image path, input quality, absent/ambiguous fields and true/near-duplicate/non-duplicate pairs. Have two annotators label source-grounded values, absences, ambiguity and entity identity, then adjudicate disagreements. Report per-field precision/recall/F1, unsupported-value and false-fill rates, missing-field detection precision/recall, and duplicate precision/recall/F1 and false-merge rate by language and input path. Include a review threshold and confidence intervals; decide risk-based acceptable thresholds before using the feature. (Collector c, “App-specific evaluation protocol”; collector a, “App-specific duplicate and cost evaluation method”; Binette et al., entity-centric framework, abstract.) [Entity-centric evaluation](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.05622)
- **App boundary (inference):** The AI may surface possible duplicates for human comparison; it may not merge records. Missingness should remain explicit and reviewable, not filled by a plausible guess. There is no evidence here that missing-field detection can currently be relied on.

#### 4. Reply drafts need factual and social review

- **Source finding:** Microsoft Research's user studies found replies that were over-positive, dissonant, culturally or gender-assumptive, or contextually impolite; selection rates alone can miss these problems. A clinical inbox study found iterative clinician/patient review improved measured acceptance in that setting, but patients judged only 77% of final-set drafts to completely address the question and identified AI authorship only 24% of the time. The authors caution about representativeness and generalization. (Robertson et al., “I Can’t Reply with That,” study findings; JAMIA, “Prompt engineering on leveraging large language models in generating response to InBasket messages,” patient preference/authorship and discussion.) [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/i-cant-reply-with-that-characterizing-problematic-email-reply-suggestions/) · [JAMIA study](https://academic.oup.com/jamia/article/31/10/2263/7717344)
- **Source finding:** EnronSR offers a reply benchmark for when to respond, length, sentiment and semantic meaning, but its abstract says its GPT-3 case study gives no guarantee of response quality or safety. FECT evaluates factual grounding of interpretive claims about contact-center conversations, using synthetic transcripts; it does not establish reply quality for this app. (EnronSR, abstract and publication metadata; FECT repository, data and evaluation sections.) [EnronSR](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/ICWSM/article/view/31448) · [FECT](https://github.com/cresta/fect/)
- **Synthesis and app boundary:** Fluency and user selection do not establish factual or contextual suitability. AI may draft; a person must check factual fit, tone, names, commitments and context, then explicitly approve sending. Do not make sending available to the drafting call. The studies are clinical, controlled, older or otherwise context-specific; none establishes quality for Bernardo's target use.

### 1.3 Where sources diverge

- **Structured format versus meaning:** Provider docs show schema constraints can reduce output-shape variability; the same docs explicitly warn that schema-valid output can be semantically wrong, incomplete or refused. Resolve by treating syntax, grounding, completeness and refusal as separate checks, not by choosing one claim over the other. (OpenAI Structured Outputs, “Structured Outputs vs JSON mode,” “Handling mistakes,” refusal handling; Google Structured Outputs, “Limitations.”)
- **Multilingual/visual capability versus transfer:** XFUND includes Portuguese and other languages; CC-OCR documents substantial language and orientation variation. These do not contradict: XFUND establishes benchmark coverage, while CC-OCR measures varying performance on different tasks. Neither warrants assuming multilingual screenshot accuracy in the app. (XFUND, dataset description; CC-OCR, language composition, multilingual variation and orientation failures.)
- **Reply acceptance versus readiness:** The JAMIA study reports positive acceptance in one clinical workflow, while Microsoft and CHI studies document contextually problematic drafts and differing recipient perceptions. Different populations, tasks and study designs explain why acceptance does not settle safety or suitability. User review remains necessary, with factual and contextual review criteria. (JAMIA, validation and discussion; Microsoft Research, findings; Liu et al., CHI 2022, abstract, interpersonal context and limitations.)
- **Duplicate reports versus benchmark false positives:** BPID demonstrates false-positive matches in its identity benchmark; Paperless-ngx issue #1688 does not establish a false positive, and discussion #1992 resolves a case as a true file-hash duplicate. The tracker reports therefore do not rebut semantic-matching risk; they are different systems and matching methods. No source gives this app's error rate or merge threshold.
- **Provider retention terms:** The sources describe different service families and features, not conflicting universal provider policies. OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini Developer API and Google Cloud terms must remain separate. Do not summarize them as one “provider retention” rule or assume ZDR by default.
