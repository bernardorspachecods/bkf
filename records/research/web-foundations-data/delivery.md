# Data: how information is modeled and stored when records have history, relate and get merged

Objective served: a guide that supports Bernardo and the agents building the app when they decide how information is modeled and stored, and when they judge a change to the model. Mode: focused on the priorities. Architecture owns how the app is divided into parts; this guide covers only how its information is modeled, and the Architecture guide points back here.

Findings are marked **Source** (what a cited source says), **Synthesis** (a pattern across sources) or **Inference** (a modeling consequence drawn from the evidence). Locators such as `A15` and `B-S21` are explained in the Source key at the end, with links; line numbers refer to the collected copy of that source, so the section or page named beside them is the one to search in the original.

## 1. The answer

The evidence is product documentation, practitioner answers, vendor guides and a few papers. It describes patterns and their costs; it does not measure them. For each priority below the sources agree on the shape of the decision and disagree on the winner, so the guide gives decisions and trade-offs, not a prescribed model.

### 1.1 Decisions to settle first

A synthesis across the six views, ordered so that each narrows the next. It is not any source's own list.

1. **What is a customer and what is a request, and can one request involve several parties?** (view 1.2)
2. **When two customers are the same, which one survives, which field values win, and what happens to the losing identifier?** Treat matching, survivorship and identity persistence as separate decisions. (view 1.3)
3. **Which values and relationships must keep point-in-time meaning, and on which time axis (when it was true, or when it was recorded)?** (view 1.4)
4. **Which fields may be empty, in which states, how is "unknown" told from "not applicable", and how are wrong-but-plausible values corrected?** (view 1.5)
5. **What is the grain of each figure the business will ask for, and does reporting run on the operational model or on a separate one?** (view 1.6)
6. **How does a legacy spreadsheet reach the model: staged and profiled first, or loaded directly, and what happens to rows that fail?** (view 1.7)

### 1.2 One customer with several requests: model the relationship at the request grain

| Approach | What the sources say | Structural limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A request row references its customer (one-to-many) | **Source:** PostgreSQL constraints §5.5.5, lines 242–260 (A1); Prisma Relations, lines 22–38 (A9); Django Models, lines 269–306 (A7). **Synthesis:** each request is queried and constrained independently, and the referencing key needs an index for the real filters (A1, lines 316–375; Drizzle, lines 595–660, A8). | One customer key per request cannot express a request owned by several customers. |
| Embed requests inside the customer document | **Source:** MongoDB embedding, lines 43–81 (S13), suits children read with the parent; unbounded arrays, lines 43–49, 77–88 (S14), describe growth and subset-query costs. | A growing embedded list makes independent request queries and updates harder and can exceed document limits. |
| Many-to-many association, with or without an explicit association model | **Source:** PostgreSQL junction tables §5.5.5, lines 294–315 (A1); Prisma many-to-many, lines 297–320 (A10); Rails Associations, lines 811–865, 979–1023 (A12) separate a bare join from an explicit association model when the link carries data. The customer/delivery/offers case (A13, question lines 70–72, answer lines 107–119) puts rate and possibly date or status on the association, and notes that repeated offers from the same pair affect the key choice. That is a low-vote case: it does not establish that every request should be many-to-many and does not cleanly separate the request from each offer. | A junction adds a key and a join; a bare implicit association cannot hold facts about the link, and a timestamp on the row does not preserve earlier versions of the link. |

**Inference:** for one customer holding several independently tracked requests, start the comparison from a customer key on the request, and add an explicit association model only if a request can relate to several parties or the link carries facts or repeated events. The anchor for the second half is the single low-vote A13 case above, and repeated events on the same pair also change which key identifies the link, so treat it as a prompt to check the grain, not a rule. A generic `(type, id)` link is not equivalent: the DBA answers disagree on alternatives, but the warning is weaker foreign-key enforcement and harder joins, while a typed supertype adds structure (A14, answer lines 93–97, 132–157). ORM relation declarations and database-enforced keys are separate things; Prisma's emulated relations add queries and do not cover raw queries (A8, A9, A12).

### 1.3 Duplicate customers: matching, survivorship, persistence and identity are separate decisions

**Synthesis.** Four product sources repeat one merge shape: pick a survivor, choose values for conflicting fields, move related records onto the survivor. Dataverse keeps the secondary inactive and links its items to the primary (B-S21, lines 63–86); CiviCRM transfers links, lets users choose field values, then deletes the duplicate (B-S23, lines 278–316); HubSpot combines associations and lets users choose properties (B-S24, lines 64–70, 86–92); FamilySearch recreates relationships against the survivor and redirects the duplicate ID (B-S22, lines 35–61). A repeated product pattern, not a database standard. Oracle's guide keeps "which record survives?" apart from "which value wins?" (C, printed p. 334, lines 12786–12795; pp. 339–340).

| Persistence approach | What the sources say | Structural limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reparent child rows to the survivor, then remove the duplicate | **Source:** A15 (lines 73–98, 138–220) shows child-uniqueness collisions and a set-based re-link with stated limits; A16 (lines 111–115, 131–153) fills missing survivor fields, reassigns dependents and deletes in one transaction; the Oracle service example moves service requests to the master (C, "move references", lines 46–56). | Every dependent relationship and unique collision must be handled, and the losing identifier is lost unless something else keeps it. |
| Keep or deactivate the loser with a pointer to the survivor | **Source:** Dataverse keeps the secondary inactive (B-S21); Salesforce sets `MasterRecordId` on the deleted loser (C, lines 23–39). **Inference:** old IDs can resolve if lookups follow the pointer; inactive rows stay visible to constraints and queries unless consistently filtered (soft-delete critique, C, lines 7–12). | The pointer does not move foreign keys or make every reader resolve the loser. |
| Cross-reference or merged-ID lookup | **Source:** Siebel stores external IDs with a source-system namespace (C, lines 5–17); HubSpot has a merged-ID property and may assign a new ID by default (B-S24, lines 108–117, 152–163). | Preserves lookup paths at the cost of a lookup step and explicit current-survivor rules; it does not reattach requests. |
| Link as duplicates without merging | **Source:** Oracle supports a registry link and leaves parties, child entities and transactions unchanged (C, "link records without merging", lines 4–6). **Inference:** counts and workflows meaning "one customer" must traverse the link to avoid double counting. | Linked records stay separate operational entities unless every consumer applies the link. |

**Identity divergence.** Sources disagree on whether the survivor keeps its ID, the loser is deactivated or deleted, the loser redirects, or a new ID is issued with old-ID lookup (B-S21–S24). HubSpot's primary-ID preservation is a beta option; CiviCRM says its merge cannot be reversed. No source supports a universal identifier policy.

**Matching is not persistence.** Fellegi–Sunter uses match, possible-match and non-match regions with error trade-offs and blocking limits (a Duke lecture reproduction, not the 1969 paper: C, slides 4–10, 13–14). Splink separates deduplication from cross-dataset linkage, requires unique input IDs (settings dictionary, lines 281–300) and recommends pair and cluster evaluation (edge evaluation, lines 201–243; cluster evaluation, lines 201–225). OpenRefine clustering is syntactic, so a candidate is not proof of identity (C, "Clustering Methods In-depth"). The Ministry of Justice record says clerical labels vary by reviewer and are not ground truth (C, MoJ Splink use, "Model performance", lines 256–258). **Inference:** duplicate detection should produce reviewable candidates; a score or cluster does not decide field survivorship, request reassignment or old-ID behavior.

**Limits of the evidence.** The generic relational merge case (A15) omits multi-column foreign keys, child unique constraints, several duplicates and concurrent activity. Product pages cover their own supported records, not custom relations or external consumers, and no source gives a reversible application merge design. A transaction protects only the rows it includes; it does not resolve child collisions or concurrent-merge policy (**Inference** from A15 and A16).

### 1.4 Retaining versions: choose what is versioned and which time is queried

| Approach | What the sources say | Structural limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Current row plus a separate history table | **Source:** A20, lines 152–168. | Historical reads need a second lookup; current and history must be kept consistent. |
| All versions in one table, flagged current or bounded by effective dates | **Source:** A18, lines 49–58; A19, lines 177–203: closing an interval and inserting a row simplifies "as of" reads. | The current version must be identifiable; interval versions add update, index and temporal-join work. |
| Stable identity with version rows, or version only selected properties | **Source:** Fowler Temporal Object, lines 74–106 (B-S4); Temporal Property, lines 74–83 (B-S5). **Inference:** version only what needs independent history. | Callers must resolve identity and interval; Fowler marks these pages draft and unmaintained since the mid-2000s. |
| Copy a referenced value onto the dependent event row | **Source:** A21, lines 144–161: simpler reads, duplicated values, harder global corrections; whether to rewrite a historical value after correction is left as a domain decision. | The copy can diverge from corrected source data. |
| Version the many-to-many relationship for point-in-time joins | **Source:** A22, lines 80–85, 100 onward: valid-time rows on the relationship with surrogate version keys. | One warehouse case, not an operational-app prescription. |
| System-versioned history or valid-time periods | **Source:** SQL:2011 paper, pp. 1–2, 4–7 (B-S8); SQL Server temporal tables, lines 33–47, 77–88 (B-S9). | System time says when the database recorded a state, which may differ from when it was true; behavior is implementation-specific. |
| Append-only event sequence | **Source:** Fowler Event Sourcing, lines 74–118, 137–186 (B-S7); Microsoft's pattern guide (C, lines 34–35, 145–148): replay needs snapshots as it grows and it may not justify its overhead for plain CRUD. | Reads depend on projections; schema evolution, concurrency and integrations add complexity. |
| Type 2 slowly changing dimension, and hybrid Type 6/7 | **Source:** Kimball, PDF pp. 14–15, lines 467–480 (B-S10): new surrogate key and effective dates per version; Type 1 overwrites history and can force aggregates to be recomputed; Type 6/7 supports "as-was" and "as-is" views. | Warehouse guidance, not a prescription for the operational model; hybrids add model and ETL complexity. |

**Synthesis.** "Never lose old versions" does not choose a temporal model. The decision includes which values and relationships need point-in-time meaning and which time axis answers the question. **Divergence:** practitioner cases prefer different shapes (a separate table keeps the live table simple but adds a lookup; same-table versions complicate the current-row query; a current flag keeps a compact live table per one answer, others stress larger tables and relationship complexity: A18, A20 lines 176–184). These are case opinions, not measurements. The audit and version policy is out of scope here and belongs to the Business logic area.

### 1.5 Incomplete or wrong fields: represent absence and completion state explicitly

| Approach | What the sources say | Structural limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Nullable fields in the same row, with conditional constraints for completed states | **Source:** a `CHECK` accepts a NULL result, so `NOT NULL` or a state-aware check is needed (A1 §5.5.1, lines 92–101); Django separates database `null` from form `blank` (A7, lines 135–145); `save()` does not call `full_clean()` (A31, lines 226–264); A32–A33 compare state-aware checks with permissive rows. | Conditional rules must stay consistent across constraints and any state-changing code. |
| Separate draft or error table until valid | **Source:** A32, lines 105–126; A36, lines 72–100, 132–177. **Inference:** a strict main table is easier to query, but promotion and "latest valid" logic must be designed. | The boundary needs coordination of related records, correction status and current-valid selection. |
| Permissive rows with completeness views or predicates | **Source:** A33, lines 103–108, 126–156: view cost depends on query patterns and the share of qualifying rows. **Inference:** consumers choose their completeness level without blocking entry. | Predicates can drift across views, constraints and jobs, and partial rows stay visible to queries that omit the filter. |
| Nullable value plus a status (unknown, not applicable) | **Source:** A34, lines 218–298: NULL alone does not give the reason; sentinel values must be filtered from counts and averages. | Status and value must be constrained to agree; reports must define each status's effect on aggregates. |

**Conditional uniqueness.** NULL behavior differs by engine and ORM: PostgreSQL treats NULLs as distinct (A1 §5.5.3, lines 156–213); Prisma allows nullable `@unique` but needs mandatory fields for compound `@@unique` (B-S16, lines 1682–1693, 1825–1858; issues B-S17, B-S18); SQL Server and MySQL differ (B-S20). Implementation details to check if the constraint is needed.

**Divergence.** One answer favors state-aware constraints and transitions, another permissive rows and views (A32–A33), from partial practitioner discussions without benchmarks. **Synthesis:** constraints enforce domain, requiredness and cross-field rules, but a plausible value can still be wrong (A35, lines 107–112, 137–142). The sources do not say how to detect a plausible real-world error or whether to keep the previous value after correction.

### 1.6 Counts and sums across periods, channels and groups: declare the grain before joining

| Approach | What the sources say | Structural limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Query normalized records at their native grain | **Source:** PostgreSQL §7.2 (A5, lines 25–33, 419–493); a fanout example inflates a customer-level sum from 8 to 10 after a join (A23, lines 111–158); Looker documents pre-aggregating or declaring cardinality (A24, joins lines 383–409, symmetric aggregates lines 204–209, 510–523). | Plain joins and `GROUP BY` do not preserve a measure's original grain. |
| Atomic fact rows with dimensions | **Source:** Kimball, PDF pp. 3–6, 10, 12–15 (B-S10): declare the grain, store atomic events, group by dimensions; an unknown/to-be-determined date row (p. 10, lines 332–345) lets an undated fact exist. **Inference:** reports must keep that category visible. | Does not define the app's metric grain or which dimensions are correct. |
| Pre-aggregate or materialize known summaries | **Source:** PostgreSQL materialized views, lines 33–54, and refresh, lines 19–41 (A6): faster repeat reads, possibly stale; Use The Index, Luke, lines 14–20, 50–63 (A25). | Freshness and refresh work are traded for speed; a pre-aggregated grain can block unanticipated questions. |
| Separate analytical store from the transactional model | **Source:** Azure workload guidance, lines 188–193, and OLAP guidance, lines 70–104, 128–131 (A26); ICPE paper, lines 35–55, 70–89 (A27): design reasoning, not a benchmark. | Adds synchronization, cleansing, orchestration and freshness management; no source gives a break-even size. |
| Same-store hybrid | **Source:** SQL Server columnstore, lines 33–49, 71–83 (A28): less ETL and latency, with index overhead; not a replacement for multi-source integration. | SQL Server-specific. |

No collected source gives app-specific workload measurements or a threshold for separating analytics. Grouping sets and materialized views are tools, not an answer to the model's grain.

### 1.7 Legacy spreadsheets: preserve an inspectable source shape before normalization when useful

| Approach | What the sources say | Structural limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stage source-shaped rows, profile, clean and map, then load | **Source:** DBA cases move spreadsheets through staging into entity and mapping tables (A29, lines 70–73, 103–105; A30, lines 103–104); Kimball ETL describes profiling, cleansing and correct/reject/load choices (B-S11, lines 33–46, 88–89); Microsoft's staging approach isolates errors but targets larger Dataverse migrations (C, lines 43–71). | Extra writes and mapping code; staging does not settle ambiguous identity or business meaning. |
| Transform in memory, then bulk-load | **Source:** Kimball contrasts direct transformation with staging, which gives archive and restart at extra write cost (B-S12, lines 51–69); PostgreSQL `COPY` maps columns, handles headers and nulls, and can skip conversion-error rows (A4, lines 112–153, 245–251). | `COPY` is transport only; it does not profile or validate business meaning, and skipped rows need review. |
| Clean and reconcile in an inspection tool, then export | **Source:** OpenRefine imports one worksheet at a time, leaves the source unchanged and keeps candidate decisions in reconciliation (C, "Starting a project", lines 26–65; reconciliation, lines 25–38, 77–96, 121–165). | A cleanup workspace is not the canonical model or migration pipeline; joining files needs separate work. |

**Synthesis.** Profile the source and decide which rows to correct, reject, quarantine or load as-is before mapping. Great Expectations can check schema and return failing rows, but schema checks do not establish semantic correctness (C, lines 61–80, 299–302, 39–52). One reported 260,000-row OpenRefine import failure is a troubleshooting case, not a product limit (C, forum thread, lines 5–23). These sources support design choices, not an import runbook.

### 1.8 Where sources diverge

1. **Duplicate identity.** Four product sources repeat survivor-plus-association consolidation but differ on identifier and deletion outcomes (B-S21–S24); each of stable survivor ID, redirect, pointer, alias map or new ID has distinct consumer costs.
2. **Incomplete rows.** Conditional constraints and state transitions versus permissive rows and views (A32–A33); costs are conditional and unbenchmarked.
3. **History shape.** Current/history tables, same-table versions, property histories, temporal periods, event sourcing, copied event-time values and valid-time relationship versions answer different questions; no measured winner.
4. **Reporting placement.** Separate OLAP, materialized summaries and same-store hybrids trade freshness and efficiency against write, refresh and operational complexity; no app-specific volume or break-even.
5. **Import staging.** Staging buys inspectability and restart at the cost of storage and transformation work; the comparisons come from warehouse ETL and specific migrations, not a small-app import.

## 2. Weak points and suggested next step

- **Merge evidence is product-specific.** A15 excludes several constraints and concurrency cases, and the app's merge and undo rules, external-reference inventory and identifier policy are not established.
- **Partial-field evidence is the thinnest.** Several entries are partial reads or community proposals with no performance data, and nothing explains how to detect plausible factual errors or keep corrected prior values.
- **History sources are mixed.** Draft pattern pages, old practitioner discussions, vendor implementations, one case for copying event-time values and one warehouse case for versioned relationships. They compare shapes but do not say which fields, relationships or time axes the app needs.
- **Reporting sources have no app-specific numbers:** no metrics, query volumes or break-even workload; the ICPE paper is a short design discussion; the hybrid option is SQL Server-specific.
- **Import sources** do not specify column mappings or how to treat ambiguous legacy values; warehouse ETL advice is an analogy.
- **Out of scope as directed:** test data, retention and privacy, audit and version rules, release migration execution, backups and restores, and database-product choice.

**Suggested next step (writer's inference from the weak points).** The gaps that block a decision sit outside this area: the audit and version policy (Business logic), retention and privacy for merged and imported personal data (Security and privacy), test data for merge and import cases (Testing), and migration in a release (Delivery). Inside this area, the open question worth a later, narrower objective is how to detect and correct plausible but wrong values while keeping what was recorded before.
