# Architecture: how a small web app is divided and how its parts communicate

Objective served: a guide that supports Bernardo and the agents building the app when they decide how it is divided into parts and how those parts communicate, now and when the structure has to change. Mode: broad.

## 1. The answer

The evidence is a set of vendor guidance, practitioner essays, provider contracts and company self-reports, not comparative studies. Across sources, the choices are about the **strength of a boundary**, and every stronger boundary costs more to operate. Claims below are what the sources say. Where the digest drew a conclusion that no source states, it is marked **Inference**.

How the digest merged the sources: Fowler's several essays count once, as one practitioner lineage. GitLab's handbook, docs, proposals and incidents count as one organizational change trail. For the main deployment-boundary trade-off there are ten origin lineages (AWS, Azure, Google Cloud, Twelve-Factor, Fowler, Thoughtworks, Segment, Shopify, 37signals, GitLab), which is ten origins, not ten comparative studies.

### 1.1 Decisions to settle first

This ordering is a synthesis across patterns, contracts and experience reports (not a source's own list); each decision narrows the boundary that follows. The sections below hold the evidence for each: decisions 1, 2 and 5 in 1.3 and 1.4; decision 3 in 1.2; decisions 4 and 6 in 1.2, 1.3 and 1.5.

1. Which requests need a result during the request, and which can be accepted for later work?
2. Which work must be scheduled, retried, resumed, or reported through a status resource?
3. Which business capabilities and public interfaces should be modules inside the application, with dependencies directed inward?
4. Which process roles need to run separately for workload, recovery, security or operational reasons while staying on one release?
5. What does each queue, webhook, API and provider contract guarantee about acknowledgment, duplicates, order, timeout, idempotency, retry, status and manual recovery?
6. What specific pressure would justify turning a process role or module into an independently deployed service, and can the team provision, observe, deploy and trace it?

**Small-team decision practice.** Record consequential boundary choices as short architecture decision records (context, decision and status, consequences, rationale) in the repository. Nygard argues that compact, modular records help later readers recover why a choice was made; practitioner guidance, not a controlled comparison. Source: [Nygard, "Documenting Architecture Decisions", lines 7–59](https://cognitect.com/blog/2011/11/15/documenting-architecture-decisions).

### 1.2 Application shape: one deployable, modules, tiers, or services

| Option | What the boundary means | Fits when | Costs or failure points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monolith | One application and deployable, code organized in-process. | A small application or team values a simple release; boundaries are not yet understood. | A change or scaling need can affect the whole application; without internal rules, modules entangle. |
| Modular monolith | One deployable with cohesive modules, explicit interfaces, controlled dependency direction. | Stronger ownership and change boundaries are needed, independent deployment is not. | Needs discipline, domain knowledge and enforcement; an in-process interface does not guarantee modularity. |
| N-tier / separate frontend and backend | A runtime or release boundary between tiers, usually an API contract. | Frontend and backend need some independence in work or scaling. | API contracts and coordinated changes can lengthen delivery; a tier split does not create business-capability boundaries. |
| Web plus worker roles | Web handles requests; a worker handles asynchronous or scheduled jobs, possibly from the same codebase and release. | Work is slow, scheduled or bursty, or needs different scaling, credentials, recovery or availability. | Needs worker lifecycle management, queue and schedule monitoring, and handling of retries, overlap, shutdown, poison work and handoff failure. Separate roles alone do not make separate services. |
| Independently deployed services | Multiple processes with network APIs or messages, often aligned to capabilities. | A component has a concrete need for independent scaling, release, security, fault isolation or ownership that one deployable cannot give. | Network failures and latency, versioned contracts, distributed consistency, cross-service testing and tracing, provisioning and operational ownership; fine-grained services multiply coordination. |

**Synthesis:** a progression of boundary strength, not a mandatory sequence or service-count target. Start with one deployable, make internal modules explicit, run different process roles where workload needs differ, and extract a service only when independent qualities or ownership justify the network and operational boundary. A web/worker split is a runtime choice, not automatically a microservice architecture. AWS describes a monolith as a reasonable small-app start; Fowler argues for delaying service separation while boundaries are uncertain; Azure and Google describe the benefits of decoupling and its costs, and Azure warns against overly granular services. Sources: [AWS, "Comparing application architectures", lines 7–27](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/prescriptive-guidance/latest/micro-frontends-aws/micro-frontend-alternatives.html); [Fowler, "Microservices", lines 132–164, 286–299, 322–330](https://martinfowler.com/articles/microservices.html); [Azure, "Microservices architecture style", lines 31–78](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/guide/architecture-styles/microservices); [Google Cloud Well-Architected, lines 354–374](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/framework?hl=en).

**Modular structure in practice.** Shopify reports strengthening business-concept boundaries and public component interfaces inside one application, enforced by CI checks, and cites infrastructure, network, testing and cross-service refactor costs as reasons not to distribute every component. GitLab's design documents describe cohesive modules and adapters that depend inward toward domain APIs. Large-company accounts, not proof that one scheme fits every app. Sources: [Shopify, "Deconstructing the Monolith", lines 84–137](https://shopify.engineering/deconstructing-monolith-designing-software-maximizes-developer-productivity); [Shopify, "Under Deconstruction", lines 243–256](https://shopify.engineering/shopify-monolith); [GitLab, "Hexagonal Rails Monolith", lines 3586–3602](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/engineering/architecture/design-documents/modular_monolith/hexagonal_monolith/); [GitLab, transport layer adapters, lines 3585–3656](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/engineering/architecture/design-documents/modular_monolith/transport_layer/).

**Experience counterweight.** Segment reports that service isolation and observability first helped, but destination-specific queues, workers, repositories and duplicated transformations became operationally costly as destinations multiplied, and it returned to a monolithic worker while accepting weaker isolation. Basecamp/37signals reports a small team keeping a broad product in a monolith for understandability and coordination. Shopify reports selective extraction for high-throughput read-only rendering and sensitive card vaulting. These show possible outcomes and motives, not thresholds or independent cost measurements. Sources: [Segment, "To Microservices and Back Again", lines 216–221, 254–289, 306–330](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/microservices-monolith-antipatterns/); [37signals, "The Majestic Monolith", lines 55–103](https://medium.com/signal-v-noise/the-majestic-monolith-29166d022228); [Shopify, "Under Deconstruction", lines 250–256](https://shopify.engineering/shopify-monolith).

### 1.3 Communication inside the application and across runtime roles

| Form | Useful when | Main trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| In-process call through a module interface | Work is immediate and modules share a process. | Low transport overhead, but boundaries erode unless dependencies are constrained and tested; moving to a network API later needs coarser contracts and different failure handling. |
| Synchronous HTTP/API call | The caller needs an immediate answer or validation result. | Simple request/reply, but caller availability, latency, timeout and retry depend on the callee; retrying a non-idempotent operation can repeat side effects. |
| Queue or point-to-point message | Producer and consumer can act at different times; work needs buffering, retry or independent scaling. | Decouples timing and can isolate failures, but adds acknowledgements, duplicate delivery, ordering and poison-message decisions, monitoring and broker operations. |
| Publish/subscribe event | Several independent consumers need a copy of an event. | Supports fan-out and decouples producers, but increases versioning, ordering, duplicate, security and subscriber-failure concerns. |
| Durable workflow / process manager | A business process spans long waits, retries, branching or several side effects. | Durable state, timers and recovery make progress observable, but need workflow design, versioning and careful treatment of replay, compensation and external effects. |

**Synthesis:** the style should follow whether the caller needs an immediate result and whether work must survive process failure or wait for a schedule. An asynchronous message says work was handed off, not that the business effect completed exactly once. Queue acknowledgement, retry and external side effects are separate steps. Sources: [AWS Well-Architected, "Loose coupling", lines 10–42](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/wellarchitected/2023-04-10/framework/rel_prevent_interaction_failure_loosely_coupled_system.html); [EIP messaging patterns, lines 101–116](https://www.enterpriseintegrationpatterns.com/patterns/messaging/); [Microsoft, "Publisher-Subscriber pattern", lines 54–124](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/patterns/publisher-subscriber).

**Runtime separation does not need a new application boundary.** Twelve-Factor describes web and worker process types running from the same release and configuration. Django's task contract and Rails Active Job separate task declaration from execution infrastructure; Laravel documents long-lived queue workers that must be supervised and restarted. Azure lists independent scaling, recovery, security controls and release cadence as possible reasons to separate job compute, and notes added hosting cost. Sources: [Twelve-Factor, "Processes" and related factors](https://12factor.net/processes); [Django Tasks, lines 35–70, 108–111](https://docs.djangoproject.com/en/6.0/ref/tasks/); [Rails Active Job, lines 90–99, 136–163, 944–1003](https://guides.rubyonrails.org/active_job_basics.html); [Azure, "Best practices for background jobs", lines 49–81, 195–257](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/best-practices/background-jobs); [Laravel Queues, lines 189–198, 5297–5458](https://laravel.com/framework/docs/queues).

**Reliability is part of the choice.** A database write followed by a failed queue publish can strand work; Azure points to a transactional outbox. Schedules and delivery can replay or overlap, so idempotent handlers matter. Long jobs may need checkpoints, graceful shutdown, retry limits, poison-message handling and queue-age monitoring. Broker guarantees are product-specific: RabbitMQ confirms and consumer acknowledgements cover different steps; JetStream adds persistence and replay to Core NATS; Kafka says exactly-once effects in external systems need cooperation from those systems. Sources: [Azure Web-Queue-Worker, lines 33–74](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/guide/architecture-styles/web-queue-worker); [Azure Background Jobs, lines 195–257](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/best-practices/background-jobs); [RabbitMQ confirms, lines 50–88, 221–346, 408–448](https://www.rabbitmq.com/docs/confirms); [NATS JetStream, lines 17–37](https://docs.nats.io/concepts/jetstream); [Kafka delivery semantics, lines 322–351, 376–378, 421–427](https://kafka.apache.org/42/design/design/).

### 1.4 External intake and outbound systems: where the app ends

**Boundary pattern.** External protocols and providers can end at an adapter that validates and authenticates input, translates it into an application request or event, and calls an inward-facing domain interface. The adapter can be an in-process module or a separate process; a separate one is justified only if its independent deployment, scaling, recovery, security or ownership outweighs its monitoring and failure costs. GitLab's transport design uses HTTP, API, GraphQL and Sidekiq as outer adapters; AWS's anti-corruption layer can be a class inside the monolith or an independent service. A synthesis of patterns, not a claim that every integration needs an adapter service. Sources: [GitLab transport layer, lines 3585–3656](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/engineering/architecture/design-documents/modular_monolith/transport_layer/); [AWS, "Anti-corruption layer pattern", lines 6–46](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/prescriptive-guidance/latest/cloud-design-patterns/acl.html); [Newman, "Microservices for Greenfield?", lines 20–28, 49–67](https://samnewman.io/blog/2015/04/07/microservices-for-greenfield/).

**Incoming requests, email and webhooks.** If the caller needs a quick result, respond synchronously; for long jobs the API can answer HTTP `202`, expose a status resource, and use an idempotency key so a retry after a lost response finds the existing operation (`202` is not a promise of completion). Email can arrive by provider callback, mailbox polling, or cloud receiving rules, and provider contracts differ in authentication, acknowledgement timing and redelivery, so provider-specific handling belongs at the edge with deduplication before business effects. **Inference:** validate and authenticate, durably enqueue or persist the callback, acknowledge promptly, and let a worker do the longer work. GitLab's own history shows the choice between a separate intake process, polling inside the job system, and webhooks is still moving (see divergence 3). Sources: [RFC 9110 §15.3.3 and §9.2.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9110.html); [Azure, "Asynchronous Request-Reply", lines 49–65, 84–109](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/patterns/asynchronous-request-reply); [AWS SES, receiving email concepts, lines 20–43, 85–114, 133–135](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/ses/latest/dg/receiving-email-concepts.html); [Stripe Webhooks, lines 493–503, 511–533, 577–594, 653–693](https://docs.stripe.com/webhooks); [GitHub, best practices, lines 59–74](https://docs.github.com/en/webhooks/using-webhooks/best-practices-for-using-webhooks); [Postmark, integration guidance, lines 174–202](https://www.postmarkapp.com/developer/integration/official-libraries); [GitLab Service Desk configuration, lines 1013–1017, 1044–1098](https://docs.gitlab.com/user/project/service_desk/configure/).

**Outgoing messages and provider calls.** The app can enqueue a send and let a worker call the provider, which may only confirm acceptance; delivery, bounce and failure arrive later as separate events that need correlation. After a timeout the sender may not know whether the effect happened, so retries need provider idempotency where offered, application-level deduplication, or a reconciliation path. Transient faults call for timeouts, finite retries with backoff and jitter, respect for `429` and `Retry-After`, and a circuit breaker for sustained failure. A GitLab SMTP timeout incident reported four copies after retries: an illustration of ambiguity, not a failure rate. Sources: [Postmark send API, lines 127–149, 212–217, 258–264](https://postmarkapp.com/developer/user-guide/send-email-with-api); [Mailgun send API, lines 175–199, 427–452](https://documentation.mailgun.com/docs/mailgun/api-reference/send/mailgun/messages/post-v3--domain-name--messages); [Stripe idempotent requests, lines 16–23](https://docs.stripe.com/api/idempotent_requests); [Azure, "Transient Fault Handling", lines 61–101, 139–151](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/best-practices/transient-faults); [Azure, "Circuit Breaker pattern", lines 33–70, 163–171](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/patterns/circuit-breaker); [RFC 6585 §4](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6585.html); [GitLab issue #410336](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-org/gitlab/-/issues/410336).

**Inference:** treat the boundary with external systems as a contract boundary even when connector code stays inside the monolith. The app needs a defined meaning for "accepted", "queued", "sent" and "delivered", and a recovery path for duplicates, timeouts, provider outages and exhausted retries. Provider acceptance is not the final business outcome.

### 1.5 When a single deployable stops being enough

Sources name conditions that can justify a stronger boundary: very different request and job load; distinct availability or recovery objectives; restricted credentials or network access; different release cadence; a capability that must scale or be isolated on its own; a stable business boundary with an accountable team; repeated coordination and release delay that internal modules cannot relieve. These are candidate signals, not extraction rules. Sources: [Azure Background Jobs, lines 195–257](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/best-practices/background-jobs); [Azure Microservices, lines 57–78](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/guide/architecture-styles/microservices); [Shopify, "Under Deconstruction", lines 250–256](https://shopify.engineering/shopify-monolith); [Fowler, "Microservice Prerequisites", lines 71–84](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/MicroservicePrerequisites.html).

Fowler's prerequisites before services become practical: rapid provisioning, monitoring, rapid deployment, close developer/operations collaboration; a larger estate also needs tracing, automated deployment, product-centered teams and manageable multi-repository development. A practitioner checklist, not a formal readiness test.

**No source gives a numerical threshold** for team size, traffic or service count. The useful test is whether a specific independent quality or ownership problem is costly inside the current boundary and whether the team can operate the new boundary. Fowler records a counterargument to monolith-first for known stable boundaries, separate delivery teams or replacements, and notes the evidence is sparse. Source: [Fowler, "Monolith First", lines 78–109](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/MonolithFirst.html).

### 1.6 Where sources diverge

1. **Monolith-first versus early independent services.** AWS, Fowler, Google, Basecamp and the Shopify modular-monolith report favor staying simpler when rapid independent evolution is not yet needed. Fowler's counterargument and Shopify's selective extraction name stable boundaries, independent teams, high-throughput paths and security isolation as reasons to split earlier. Positions differ by workload, boundary confidence and team structure; none gives a measured threshold.
2. **Separate web and worker processes versus one deployable.** Azure's Web-Queue-Worker emphasizes clear responsibility, independent scaling and asynchronous decoupling; Twelve-Factor describes separate process types sharing a release. Compatible if "separate" means runtime role; they diverge only if a process split is taken to mean an independently deployed service.
3. **Internal queue versus HTTP webhook for incoming email.** GitLab's history shows direct Sidekiq enqueue, a proposal to move polling into Sidekiq, then a proposal to deprecate direct delivery for webhooks. Internal delivery avoids external ingress but couples intake to app queue details; webhooks add endpoint availability, network and authentication configuration. Proposals and deployment-specific guidance, not a completed comparison.
4. **"Reliable delivery" versus end-to-end completion.** Queue and provider docs separate publisher, broker, consumer and external-side-effect acknowledgments. At-least-once allows duplicates; accepted or queued is not delivered or acted on; retrying after a timeout may repeat a completed side effect.
5. **Modular monolith as enough versus a transition stage.** Shopify presents modular boundaries as useful long-lived structure plus selective extraction; GitLab's designs describe a transition from entanglement to modularity. Not a contradiction: sources differ on product size, current boundary quality and whether independent runtime properties are needed. Neither shows a modular monolith is always sufficient.

## 2. Weak points and suggested next step

- **No comparative evidence.** There is no like-for-like comparison of monoliths, modular monoliths, web/worker deployments and services for an app like this. Company reports are self-reports; practitioner articles are experience-based; incidents illustrate failure modes, not prevalence.
- **No numeric threshold.** No source gives a traffic, headcount or service-count point for adding a deployable. The guide offers conditions and trade-offs only.
- **Thin small-team evidence.** Basecamp is a founder's report from roughly 12 programmers; Segment's team is also around 12 but the system is a large event-delivery platform; Shopify and GitLab are far larger. None is a template for a new intake-and-messaging app.
- **Public form handling** is covered mainly through the asynchronous request-reply pattern, not a case study of this product shape. Email-intake examples are specific to SES, Postmark and GitLab's Mailroom.
- **Provider behavior is provider-specific.** Stripe, GitHub, Postmark, Mailgun and SES show useful differences but do not define all webhook and email contracts. Retry schedules, authentication, rate limits, retention and redelivery can change and must be checked for the actual integration.
- **Queue and workflow products** have distinct semantics and operational costs; product docs and issue reports give no neutral basis to choose among them. "Exactly once" across external side effects is not established.
- **The first collection review** found the external intake and output boundaries under-supported; later collection added HTTP, webhook, email and provider material and the second round passed, but the provider-specific and case-study limits above remain.
- **Out of scope here:** data modeling and framework or vendor choice. They are covered by the Data area and by later work.

**Suggested next step (seed for the next objective).** The weak points concentrate in what happens after the boundary is decided: queue and workflow semantics, scheduled work, retries and duplicates, and provider contracts for email intake and sending. They belong to the Background work and Email and messaging areas, where the open questions above (exactly-once effects, provider-specific recovery, poison work and retry budgets) are the priorities. Small-team, intake-shaped evidence is the gap most worth searching for there.
